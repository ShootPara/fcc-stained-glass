[P0021] Preface
[P0022] So many questions have been asked concerning the unique and beautiful features of the new Church-its architecture, its art, and its meanings-that a book of description and interpretation seemed invaluable. We are also making a venture in a direction which may seem to depart from the old landmark, with which we have grown up, and which were nevertheless the products of an earlier age of reaction. It is for this reason that the Minister begins this book with a chapter on the "Old and the New".
[P0023] We should also look ahead to Tomorrow, to the coming. generations, and believe that they will have an interest in our present work, just as we look back with grateful hearts to the heroic and honorable history of Yesterday. We write today in the spirit with which the Reverend A. S. Kedzie wrote, in a letter dated 1852 and found in the corner stone box, "How changed will be the conditions of this place, and of this Church, when this shall be brought forth for the perusal of some distant generation."
[P0024] It has been a source of wonderment that we have been able to carry out this great building program to a triumphant conclusion. The reason is evident. Our undertaking has been purely congregational. Every organization and every individual was eager to have a part in it. Every one gave freely of his service and of his treasure. As we look back upon it all, we see how great is our debt of gratitude to the many committees in carrying on their several tasks, especially to the chairman of the Board of Trustees and the chairman of the Building Committee, upon whom the brunt of the burden has fallen. No man can count the time or measure the energy that was so freely given to plan and to execute every step of the new building from floor to spire.
[P0025] Although these labors of love and sacrifice may have borne heavily upon us, let us now rejoice as we enter at last our promised land. As our greatest satisfaction, let us remember that a Church building before which people pass by with silent admiration and awe will cast an uplifting influence upon the community for generations.
[P0026] The Old and The New
[P0027] The contrast between the massive square character of our former Church with its turrets and great tower, and the architectural novelty and splendor of the new Gothic edifice is apparent to all. The development away from the plain quaint meeting house of our Pilgrim forebears to the delicately wrought structure of today, "graven with art and man's device", is still more striking. We have gone back to the Cathedral-like temple from which they fled in fear; we have employed the forms of color and beauty which seemed to them a serious stumbling block to the religion of the spirit.
[P0028] This change signifies that something revolutionary in thought and experience has taken place. First of all the world is progressing and new ideas are bursting in upon us from every quarter. We are living in a very different world from that of our forefathers. We are more widely educated, we are enjoying more fully the fruits of culture, and we are more world-conscious. We realize that we are the "heirs of all the ages in the foremost files of time". Not so with the conditions governing the life and mind of early New England. The people were lacking in wealth, and their hardships did not permit the development of the artistic sentiment. Beauty was a vanity partly because it was costly. The simplicity of the early Colonial Church, which consisted of four walls and a stove, was an economic necessity, although it also expressed the austere and ascetic spirit of the age. In later days, however, as the colonies prospered, a type of church architecture did develop whose exterior possessed a beauty of design and character, which was one of the finest expressions of the Georgian Age. This is a noteworthy fact. For with the exception of that type, America has achieved little distinction in architecture until the present renaissance of building. From the colonial age on, mongrel types began to appear, until the barren theatre-like structure without any obvious churchly features resulted.
[P0029] Ancient as the vision of David, who refused to dwell in a house of cedar, while the Ark of God remained in a tent, has been the conviction that the House of God should be worthy of His greatness. Divinity cannot be portrayed or respected in tawdry or slovenly surroundings. While Religion is spiritual, its physical embodiment should be noble. He who is the architect of all and who inhabits eternity is worthy of the noblest habitation that man can erect. Even the ancients, whose ideas are but   the starlight of Truth, still felt that the Temple of the Great Divine Being should reflect that immeasurable grandeur which heaven and earth declare. From the distant Temple of Thebes and the Parthenon, to Amiens and St. Paul's, religion has expressed its awe, reverence and devotion by the employment of proportion, line and color on the grandest scale. For us, who can survey all the varied Temples which this believing world has erected, the Gothic style fittingly expresses the infinite God, "who clothes Himself with light as with a garment", and whose universe embodies the principal of perfection even to the tiniest atom.
[P0030] Beauty has ever been the hand-maiden of religion. Color, artistic figures and drawings may still be seen upon the mighty columns of Karnak, and upon the walls of Baalbeck. Israel's Temple blazed with gold, royal purple and jewels. The aesthetic impulse in every race consecrated all of its powers to beautifying the House of God. It was a spontaneous impulse that added to the grandeur of temples, the wondrous forms of beauty. An object of beauty weaves a holy spell about the soul. As some one has written, "The human heart aspires to the mountain tops. Our souls demand the moments of exaltation. Of the human joys, the joy of beauty is the one most universally demanded, and gives the most universal satisfaction." It is not strange, therefore, that the oldest religions consisted of nature-worship, inspired by the glorious spectacle of the rising and setting sun. While one Psalmist tells us, "Worship the Lord in the beauty of Holiness," another sings the praise of Him, "who made everything beautiful in its time." The Christian world has not always been aware of this. The Puritans were not entirely oblivious to the values of natural beauty, but the vision of God to which they clung was the God who dwelt upon the throne of judgment.
[P0031] The widening thought of today, however, beholds Him imminent within the whole universe, where stars course and flowers charm, as well as in the immutable principles of righteousness. It is a singular fact that beauty is also recognized as an asset to every practical field of life, to school, factory and theatre. Art, color, music, wealth make them alluring. An attractive structure excercises an unconscious drawing power. Can the Church afford an inartistic edifice? A day has come when unknown numbers of people whose spiritual natures crave the beautiful, decline to worship in a building violating the canons of good taste. Even the exterior of a church may excite religious feelings and thoughts. Millions in Europe who revolt from ecclesiasticism look up to the stately cathedral which they never enter and breathe their unspoken prayers. Surely that which silently appeals to the religious side of man's nature is something which should not be neglected in the construction of the House of God.
[P0032] When we come to the supreme principle of our Faith—the idea of the Church Universal-we find it too embodied. The distinctive note in this structure is the synthesis of all the valuable elements which have contributed to the great "artistic cosmos", something which has grown out of the noble Christian past, both religious and architectural, yet links itself with the life and faith of today. Likewise Congregationalism seeks the truest and best elements in every Christian Communion, the fervor and faith of the evangelical, the quest of the open mind for truth, and the beauty which hitherto has been the exclusive property of the liturgical churches. Our new Church is a visible expression of this consummate idea in brick and stone and spire without, and in arch, color and atmosphere within. Is not this the Great Church which the groping world awaits a world bewildered by the rivalry of creeds, and coveting only the peace of Christ amidst the hectic fever of modern life? The great hope with which we have built is that ours may become a great Community Church-a great force for promoting the spirit of charity and brotherly love, a great incentive to public service, a great center for beautiful music and of progressive thought, a great teacher of the gospel and a great Temple of Worship.
[P0033] The Design of the Building
[P0034] In the design of the building there were several factors which were of almost equal importance, some aesthetic, some practical, which may be summed up as follows: In the first place the position of the building on the lot was determined by a desire to have an entrance to the parish house directly on the street and a communication between the parish house and the Church at the end of the Church House opposite to the street entrance. This might have been effected in several ways, but it seemed that from the aesthetic point of view the placing of the Church at one side of the lot would give much more dignity to the Church building itself, since it would be between the wide open space on its own property and the wide open space between the property line and the Church adjoining. As the function of the whole Church building consists really of three things, first the church auditorium, second the Church School teaching rooms, and third the assembly rooms for other parish purposes, each of these functions was grouped in a separate building, but so arranged that easy and direct communication could be had between them. As will be seen the small building on Park Street includes club rooms, Church offices, and class rooms. The connecting link between that and the Church includes the assembly rooms and class rooms of the Church School. The Church building itself is a separate unit including the vestibules, the auditorium, the chancel, and the choir rooms. The Church School is divided so that each of the departments occupy a separate floor, making for simplicity of administration and complete separation of the various activities. Stairways are placed at either end of the Church School so that in case of fire or panic immediate and rapid emptying of the school rooms is possible. In the Church itself a large assembly room is placed below the auditorium for purposes of general parish meetings, with a small entrance from the easterly porch of the church. The main entrance to this assembly room is from the Church House, it being thought that the Church House entrance would be the one generally used and that the class rooms would be available for coat rooms and meeting rooms for people approaching this assembly room. Now while these are for the most part purely practical factors, they have influenced the design of the building to a degree which is perhaps not generally appreciated. The various functions required windows of different sizes and at different levels, and as it is a fundamental principle of architectural design, that no exterior is aesthetically satisfactory unless it expresses the purpose for which it is used, it will be seen that a very free style of architecture must be employed to reconcile so many various elements into an harmonious whole. The Church itself should obviously be as good an auditorium as possible, other elements considered. Obviously, the best type of auditorium is the well designed theatre. It is difficult, however, if not impossible, in an auditorium of the theatre type to have the solemnity and dignity which are essential to the services of the Christian Church. It was felt both by the committee and the architect that certain sacrifices of plan should be made to secure a genuinely ecclesiastical feeling in the building itself, and for that reason the building committee desired a Church which would recall the time when Christian architecture rose to its highest point, namely the Gothic period. It was both logical and desirable that the Church House should be treated in complete harmony with the decorative motives of Gothic architecture, and in order to accomplish this purpose, a very free adaptation of Gothic ecclesiastical design was used in the Church building and an equally free adaptation of the lay buildings of the Gothic period was used in the Church House. Practically all Gothic churches were composed of two elements, an elevated platform or chancel at one end of the Church for the minister, choir, and lectern, and the nave or auditorium for the congregation. Practically all the old English Gothic Churches were taken over by the Church of England at the time of the Reformation, and the "dissenting" Churches, desiring to emphasize the differences which existed between them and the Episcopal Church, did away with many of the liturgical elements of the established Church. The typical American Baptist, Methodist, Congregational, and Presbyterian Churches have therefore been built without the interesting and lovely chancel. These Churches felt that the chancel was characteristic of the once hated Episcopal form of worship, a thing for which there is no historical foundation and which has deprived these non-liturgical Churches of an architectural form of great beauty and which is a dignified addition to the Church services. In this Church the chancel forms an important part of the design.
[P0035] Since this is the twentieth century, and not the twelfth, the methods of building, the materials, and even the methods of design and superintendence, are vastly different from those of the Gothic builders. In the design of this building the attempt was made to do what a Gothic designer would probably have done had he been given the opportunity to use reinforced concrete floors, steel trusses, and copper for flashing, while still retaining the general spirit of the old buildings. The old English parish Church form, especially that variant of it which was adopted as a basis for the design of this building, was a well thought out expression of the needs of a congregation at divine service. The small low side aisles screened late comers from the view of the congregation as they passed to their seats, and the porches gave an opportunity for all those who could reach their seats from the side aisles, to get to them without disturbing the service. For this reason as well as because the side aisles required some architectural termination on the street, separate porches were used instead of the other entrance form, the narthex, across the street end of the church. Further, practically the full length of the lot was used, so that if a narthex were used the church itself would have had to be shortened.
[P0036] The architectural design of the exterior of the auditorium is a genuine expression of both its use and its structure. The roof is carried on steel trusses, supported by long brick piers, treated in the traditional buttress form. The walls between are comparatively light and almost entirely of glass. Over the roofs of the aisles, buttresses are carried against the piers supporting the roof, practically in the form of flying buttresses roofed over, and the thrust, or the tendency of the roof to spread outwards, is taken up in a manner, which is not only sound from an engineering point of view, but is also traditional ecclesiastically. The ceiling of the Church is formed by a series of intersecting vaults following the traditional groined vaulting of Gothic days, but treated in a manner more suited to its material than the forms developed in Gothic stone work would be. In the old Churches the arch, which to some extent separated the nave from the chancel, was called the "GLORY ARCH" and was commonly expressed on the exterior by a tower or spire of some sort; here a steel and copper fleche was substituted for a tower, because it was felt that the accent desired at this point could be better expressed by a light and open motive on a building with a steel trussed roof, than by conventional masonry. If the architect may be excused for expressing his enthusiasm about his own work, he would like to direct particularly the attention of the congregation to the delicate lace-like quality of the fleche as seen against the evening sky.
[P0037] The materials of the exterior are a brick which has a peculiarly lovely variation of color, laid in mortar of a slightly yellow color, and with the important structural points such as the window jambs, the door heads, the porches, the buttress caps, and so forth, reinforced with cast stone. In certain parts of the building the brick work is laid in an unobtrusive pattern which is not apparent except upon examination, but the absence of which would be felt unfavorably,it is one of the many places in architectural design where things are done which produce no distinct effect upon the beholder but which contribute in a subtle way to the harmony of the building. On the exterior of the building it is desired to call attention especially to the unusual treatment of the tops of the buttresses, to the finials, to the tracery of the windows, and to the proportion of the street facade, as well as to the effect of great length on the side, due to the repetition of similar motives of buttresses and windows, as one looks along the garden side.
[P0038] It will be found that the interior of the Church is an architectural expression entirely consistent in character with the exterior. The nave is free from all ornament except the structural features, but the chancel has the organ screen at the Glory Arch treated with ornamental pylons (on each side of the choir stalls), the organ front, and the wainscoting of the walls, panelled in carved walnut of a character which, while structurally reminiscent of certain of the southern French Gothic interiors, as for example, that at Albi, has no exact precedent. The wainscot is raised in the center of the rear to lend dignity to the position of the Communion table, and to give a base to the beautiful stained window which is the focal point of the whole interior. It is desired also to call attention to the treatment of the junction between the nave and the chancel, the glory arch, with a beam tying the two pylons together, reminiscent of the rood beam of the very early English churches.
[P0039] Although this is a modern Church, the spirit in which it was executed was not at all dissimilar to that displayed by the Gothic builders, when each mechanic put as much loving care into the stones he hewed as if the building had been his personal expression of worship. It has not been the good fortune of the writer to have ever designed a building where not only the contractors and subcontractors were so sincerely interested in producing exactly the quality of workmanship and material that was desired and specified by the architect, but the individual mechanics entered so whole-heartedly and with such spirit into the execution of the work. Those who are pleased with the lovely stone ornament may be interested to know that the name of the architectural sculptor who modelled them is Mr. George Karompay. The gargoyles at the heads of the buttresses, the oak and vine leaf ornaments around the door ways, the finials, are also expressions of his gay and reverent ability. The careful laying out of the building, the admirable quality of the brick work, and the excellent technical execution of many details too often taken for granted, are due to Mr. Adrian Vermeulen, the general superintendent, and Mr. William Denison, the mason foreman. Mr. Vermeulen, especially, contributed much more than he was paid for to the general spirit of the work, as did Mr. Charles A. Kipp, the carpenter foreman. The exquisite and subtle finish on the plaster work of the church was due to the intelligence and sympathetic cooperation of Mr. Benjamin TenBrink, the plastering foreman. These men, even more than the architect or contractors, contributed in giving the building that feeling of quality which one sees in all the old work, and which is so generally absent from the new, and the writer is grateful for this opportunity to express to them his appreciation.
[P0040] In the decoration of the Church there were three items of very great importance, the magnificent stained window over the communion table, the admirable colored windows with their delightful inserts along the sides, and the decoration of the ceiling and side walls. The stained window over the chancel has for its subject, in the center, Christ with his Mother, and three of the principal apostles, and below, those vital acts of His life upon which the Christian faith is founded. Above the figures of Christ and the apostles, a rich Gothic tracery in colored and painted glass soars into a sky illuminated by the star of Bethlehem. A very careful study of the window will be repaid, for in the simple main design are included many elements of symbolic interest in the Christian religion. As in the case of all ornaments used everywhere in the Church, each item has its individual significance as a symbol of some aspect of the Christian religion, as well as being an ornament which is in itself a decoration. The designer of this window is Mr. Alfred E. Floegel of New York.
[P0041] The side windows and the window at the south end were contracted for with the idea that a good quality of commercial glass was to be obtained with the inclusion of a certain number of geometrical patterns. Mr. A. L. Brink, however, entered so thoroughly into the spirit of the building that in place of the geometrical color inserts he has furnished a series of the most delightful allegorical representations of the various events described in the Bible. They run in chronological order from the creation in the first window on the right-hand side of the church near the reading desk, to the martyrdom of the disciples in the last window on the left hand side near the pulpit. The decorations of the ceiling were designed to give color and richness to the plaster vault, which, although of excellent form, would have been uninteresting without either stone ribs and fan vaulting, or some such painted decoration as was used. Here again the decorators, Miss Lucinda Goldsborough of New York assisted by Miss Jean Cotton of Baltimore, entered into the spirit of the building and gave to the work far more in time and creative effort than they had contracted to supply. Again the decorations are far from meaningless. The central band on the ceiling over the chancel is a conventional representation of the Last Supper; the first boss, or circular spot, above the altar shows the cross and lilies characteristic of Easter, while the boss at the opposite end of the Last Supper, next to the Glory Arch, represents the Christ child surrounded by a wreath indicative of the Christmas festival. At the bases of the vaulting were placed ten decorative panels representative of various Christian virtues as symbolized and expressed by some of the outstanding great figures of all time. Here again the decorative motives were composed of scenes and attitudes of mind characteristic of Christianity and a careful study of the various items in the decorations will indicate the interesting manner in which symbolism has been combined. A full list of the subjects of the various motives will be found below. The architect desires especially to express his appreciation for the work of his collaborator, Mr. Howard Young, to whose capable hands the execution of a work very much beloved by its author was necessarily entrusted, and to whom are due the admirable designs and interesting execution of the panels above the entrance doors from the porches to the Church, and the leaded panels below the large window in the minister's study in the Church House, as well as many other items not so conspicuous but equally essential to the successful execution of the work.
[P0042] It was with great reluctance that the writer felt himself obliged to forego, because of the distance between the Church and his office, the privilege of superintending the construction of the building, but he has now no feeling of regret, so completely has Mr. Young entered into the spirit of the design and so carefully has he guided the artist mechanics who erected it. Since the pastor, who will edit this book, because of his modest personality, will probably be little inclined to say a good word for himself, the architect ventures the hope that the Reverend Torrance Phelps, will see fit not to excise from this record of the building of the Church a few words of appreciation of his own helpful, tactful and enthusiastic attitude. Mr. Phelps has brought many inspirations to the designer of the building, in many cases used with enthusiasm on the part of the architect, but when certain of his enthusiastic dreams were found impossible of realization, he withdrew with kindliness and consideration. In conclusion, the architect desires to say that no building was ever well built if the client was bad, and in this particular building it was the chairman of the building committee, Mr. Robert E. Staebler, who was or represented the client; who stood between many conflicting desires, who shielded the architect from blame whether just or unjust, who was always able to be reached, who made his decisions quickly, promptly, and intelligently, and if, as the architect fondly believes, this church is a building which will be an everlasting credit both to him and to the congregation, he desires to say that no one man has so contributed to its success as the chairman of the building committee.
[P0043] The Memorial Window
[P0044] As we enter the nave and look toward the front, the first object that strikes our vision like a sunburst is the Memorial Window blazing with a gem-like iridescence. As we walk toward it and stand beneath its unrivalled splendor, we see the familiar figures of Gospel story, clothed with majesty, and surrounded by the symbols of our Christian faith and hope. The central of the five larger panels, toward which all eyes are drawn, represents Christ as the Peace Maker. In His left hand He holds a globe, on the top of which is a cross, the historic emblem of the truth that peace and goodwill come through the crucifixion of selfishness. The right hand of Jesus is upraised to bless the whole world. The sheep about His feet refer to the Good Shepherd of St. John, who was solicitous for his human flock.
[P0045] The figures on the left and right of Christ are His Mother Mary and His cousin, John the Baptist. These two people were the associates of Jesus during the early years of His life, His Mother attending Him unto the end. John the Baptist helped to prepare the Way for Jesus, when He began his public ministry. John, at the right, is clothed as the New Testament tells us in a rough garment of camel's hair. Since he spoke of Jesus as the "Lamb of God", this salutation is symbolized by the Shepherd's crook in his left hand. The Mother of Jesus in the panel to the left of Him is represented as being in the attitude of prayer. The adoring angels at the bottom of the panels at the left and at the right of Christ, partly facing Him, suggest the "Te Deum" in the words, "Heaven and earth adore Thee". The angels are also said to represent the spirits of the good in heaven in the attitude of devotion to Christ.
[P0046] St. Paul, the fearless figure in the far-left panel, holds in his right hand a book as a symbol of his calling to be a preacher to the Gentiles. It also represents his mission as a powerful writer, who sustained by his burning epistles the Christian faith of the churches he established. The sword in his left hand indicates the instrument that caused his death as a martyr to the Faith.
[P0047] The panel at the extreme right depicts Peter as a fiery and fearless leader. He is holding a scroll containing the teachings of Jesus, which he, as the appointed apostle to the Jews, was to carry to distant lands. He also holds the keys, which typify, that belief in Christ is the rock on which the Church is founded, and which opens to all the benefits of the Christian religion. Underneath these great figures are striking emblems. Below the figure of Mary is the coat of arms of the State of Michigan, while below the figure of St. John is the coat of arms of the United States of America. Beneath the painting of Jesus appears the Cross and the Latin inscription: "I. H. S." meaning I—Jesus; H—Hominum; S-Salvator: "Jesus the Saviour of Man". Sometimes it is known as "In Hoc Salus"—In this (Cross) is salvation.
[P0048] The five smaller panels the Predella-reading from left to right, present the great scenes in the life of Jesus. The first panel shows the Nativity or the birth of Christ in the stable at Bethlehem and the adoring of the Infant Saviour by the Magi. In the second panel is seen the Baptism of Christ in the river Jordan. John is performing the act. Mythical angels are in the back-ground. The third panel depicts Christ on the Cross, at the foot of which stands His Mother and His beloved disciple St. John. The fourth panel shows Christ rising from the tomb. The fifth panel depicts the Ascension of Christ into Heaven, in the sight of His disciples gazing toward their departing Master and filled with wonderment at the great event. The inscription plate below the center panel bears the name of Rachel Babcock Upjohn in whose memory the window was given. Alfred E. Floegel, who designed the window, describes the artistic features as follows: The ornamentation of the Tracery and Canopy work is in the style of English Perpendicular Gothic. In the upper section of the diamond shaped portion of the tracery is the "Star of Bethlehem", radiating its rays through the whole tracery. The color effect is brought out by light tints and golden decorations with little spots of color here and there to bring out more vividly the artistic effect of ornamentation. Directly beneath the Star of Bethlehem and in the section on either side are three heavenly spirits standing on pinnacles symbolic of their heavenly abode.

[P0162] The Murals and Panels
[P0163] Since the painted scenes on the ceiling and arches are so rich in stories of heroic figures the Minister will describe the details visible, in order that we may see and feel the great cloud of presences which surround us.
[P0164] The color scheme of the Church is designed to enrich the atmosphere and fire the soul by the suggestion of the mystical beauty of religion. The long ascending arches in variegated colors, the broad bands running along the top glowing with blues, purples and emerald, enhance the sense of height and distance. At the juncture of the rising lines are the symbolic circles, each representing a Biblical scene. While the main purpose of the color scheme is to create an interior flaming like an iridescent jewel box, nevertheless, it represents for our instruction familiar pictures. The first circle represents Jacob working seven years for his father-in-law, Laban, in order to marry Leah. The second circle portrays St. Anne teaching the Law and the Prophets to Mary, the Mother of Jesus. The third is the beautiful picture of the Good Shepherd, described in St. John's Gospel.
[P0165] A lamb lies upon the altar as the sacrificial victim, while in the background is a Cross symbolizing the fact that the Good Shepherd lays down His life for His flock. The fourth circle represents Job and the three friends who come to comfort him, but only repel him with their wrong idea of the cause of his suffering. The fifth circle pictures Herod who, bewitched by Salome, regretfully keeps his promise to her by giving her the head of John the Baptist.
[P0166] The Panels Upon the Arches
[P0167] While the main purpose here is also to add to the beauty of the color scheme, still, the pictures are designed to typify. the ten Christian virtues with worthy historical figures. Upon the East wall, beginning at the front, the first virtue is Love, whose finest expression is self-sacrificing service. Clara Barton, who organized the American Red Cross, stands before a wounded soldier, ministering to his needs. The tree typifies the Tree of Life which Love creates. The candlesticks are the lights of Hope. Humility is represented by the heroic figure of John Milton, poet, publicist and genius, bowing before no man, yet prostrating himself before the greatness of God. In the scene we see his daughter reading to him in his blindness. The canopy above upholds the Angel of Inspiration, and although blind, Milton's inward vision beholds glorious dreams. The angels climbing upward are Gabriel, Michael, a Seraphim, and an Archangel. Hope is represented by the figure of Columbus bidding his wife and child farewell before he sets forth upon his voyage. The ship and the dragon above indicate the current belief that giant sea monsters awaited to devour the adventurer.
[P0168] Charity is typified by St. Francis of Assisi, whose transparent spirit of kindness and generosity melts the fear of the animal world. At his feet lie the lion and the tiger, while on his shoulders rest the birds of the air-all of whom he called his brothers. Joy is signified by the gorgeous figure of Handel at his golden organ. In his hands is a manuscript, indicating by the notes that it may be the Messiah, which is the greatest oratorio and which shouts the joy of the advent of the Redeemer.
[P0169] Upon the right side opposite Handel is Devotion-Washington enduring the terrible privations of Valley Forge for love of his Country's welfare. Forgetting his own sufferings, he is consoling a wounded drummer boy. Above them is a coat of arms facetiously describing the manner in which many who boast of heraldry attain it. A King, who is attacked by a robber, is shown being rescued by a cook from the Inn, flourishing a spit upon which three birds are impaled. To him a crest of arms was given by the King to show his gratitude. Upon either side of this scene are the eagles, emblematic of America.
[P0170] Purity is embodied in Fra Angelico—“brother angel"—"the Heavenly painter." He is depicting the journey of the Wise Men, the Kings of the Orient, going to Bethlehem to present their gifts to the Mother and her Babe. The Shepherd's crook speaks of the Shepherds who were first to kneel before the manger where Mary, the Mother of Jesus, awaited them.
[P0171] Joan of Arc incarnates Courage. Saint, soldier, martyr, hearing the voices of the angels, pictured above her, she feels called to liberate France from her enemies. Hearing that she is coming to him, King Charles VII disguises himself as a noble to test her heavenly claims, and pushes her away with outstretched hand. But Joan of Arc, braving the pomp and scorn of the court, picks him out by his royal mien and is elected to lead the armies to victory.
[P0172] Sincerity is symbolized by Isaac Newton, the scientist, who is the impartial seeker after Truth. He is standing at his laboratory table with his experimental tubes and crucible. Above him is a tree whose fallen apple suggested to him the law of gravity. Two observatory towers on either side of the tree illustrate his astronomical studies of the stellar universe.
[P0173] Faith is fittingly represented by William Brewster, Minister, elder, leader of the Pilgrim band. He is standing on Plymouth Rock upholding, amid the storm, the faith of his wife and child, and beyond them, the faith of all that Pilgrim company. Above is the picture of a New England Church in the wilderness with two black lines, representing people entering the doors of the meeting house. The many trees we see stand for the trees of Faith, of which the psalmist sang: "by which flow the rivers of water and whose fruit never faileth.”

[P0207] The Pipe Organ
[P0208] The organ is a sermon in music. It reminds us that song was born at Bethlehem and that the organ is the grandest instrument ever devised to exalt the soul and to sound every gamut of lofty human feeling. This instrument is capable of the softest and most delicate tones, while at the same time it is capable of filling the nave with the diapasons of the sea. The pipes in front are for decoration only. The pipes which sound, of which there are two thousand one hundred twenty-one, are inside and out of sight. The organ is a product of the Austin Organ Company of Hartford Connecticut, whose instruments are famous throughout the country, among which might be mentioned the Salt Lake City Tabernacle organ-well known to tourists; the Medinah Temple, Chicago, and St. George's Episcopal Church, New York City.
[P0209] The organ builders describe our organ in the following words: The instrument is a three manual and Echo organ, having three keyboards besides the pedal keyboard, the lowest manual being the Choir Organ, the center manual the Great Organ and the upper manual the Swell Organ. The Echo Organ plays from the Great manual as it can be immediately switched on or off at the will of the organist. Therefore, there are five distinct divisions of the instrument. First, the Pedal Organ of six speaking stops, is the bass played from the pedal keys. Second is the Choir Organ of seven stops, including the Harp. All are enclosed in a swell box, rendering them expressive or capable of being made louder or softer as the swell shades are opened or closed. Third, the Great Organ of six speaking stops contains the more powerful pipes, such as the Diapasons and Tubas. These stops also, with the exception of the First Diapason, are enclosed in the Choir Organ swell box. Fourth, the Swell Organ has thirteen speaking stops, all enclosed in its own swell box.
[P0210] It is interesting to note that the name Swell Organ was given to this section of the organ, since originally it was the ONLY part of an organ enclosed in a swell box. Modern improvements, however, have tended to make the pipe organ more and more expressive. In this instrument there are three swell boxes, with everything expressive except the First Diapason of the Great Organ and some of the Pedal stops.
[P0211] One may naturally be interested in the general control of such an instrument. The action is electro pneumatic throughout, insulated wires running from every key and stop to small electromagnets placed in the organ chests, wherever located. The magnet operates a small armature valve which in turn controls air pressure to the pneumatics opening the valves which blow the pipes or strike the blows on the Chimes or Harp.
[P0212] The organ console may be looked upon as the controlling station or switch board, as the organist in all his operations is closing miniature switches, obtaining his musical results through the ingenious electro pneumatic mechanism. The console in an Austin organ is direct electric in all its mechanism, which means that no pneumatic work or air supply is required. All the electric contacts are made of a special silver alloy found to be the best and most practical metal for this purpose, insuring positive and durable results.
[P0213] One could go to great length in describing this instrument, but the specifications which follow give the various stops, couplers, etc., which musicians will be especially interested in.

[P0261] The Garden
[P0262] Christianity originated as an open air religion. The walks made by Jesus and His disciples through Judea and Galilee, teaching as they went, scattered the seed which has borne rich fruitage in every realm of life. In the gravel paths of the Sunken Garden we behold the suggestions of these walks, the walks through Samaria, the walk to Emmaus, the walks of St. Paul up and down the shores of the Mediterranean.
[P0263] To have a beautiful garden in the heart of a city is like an oasis in a desert. It not only exhales its fragrance amid the dust and grime, but also stirs that spontaneous love of Nature whose influence is always refreshing and uplifting. People who love the flowers, the shrubs and the green grass are apt to love the God, one of whose manifestations is the Beauty of the world.
[P0264] The location and names of the various trees, shrubs and flowers are described by the chairman of the Garden Committee in the paragraphs that follow.
[P0265] In the Lower Terrace one finds in the four corners of the central grass panel, four Japanese Yew trees. On the other side of the walk which surrounds the panel on the east and west sides are two varieties of Juniper, the Pfitzer and the Tamarix. Flowering Dogwood is planted in the southeast and southwest corners and also on the west side near the steps. In the Lower Terrace are planted also the following shrubs: Fragrant Mock Orange, Waxberry, Burning Bush, Panicle Hydrangea, Honeysuckles, Van Houttei Spirea, Yellow Root, Sweet Pepper Bush. One finds also Monkshood, Day Lilies, Larkspur, Phlox, Madonna Lilies. There is a ground cover of evergreen Japanese Spurge, Periwinkle, Crocus and Daffodils. On each side of the steps leading down into the Lower Terrace are two Cedar trees.
[P0266] On the Second Level, Japanese Yew trees are planted by the steps which lead into the lower terrace. By the steps coming down from the Vestibule of the Church, there is a Pfitzer Juniper and Dogwood. There is also a Scarlet Fruited Dogwood in the northern corner of this terrace. On either side of the walk are also Snowberry, Roses, and Hawthorn. In the southwest corner of this terrace is a group of three Cedars.
[P0267] Upon the Main Level, between the upper terrace wall and the side walk, and near the wall, is planted a privet hedge. As yet no trees have been planted.
[P0268] Symbolism of the Church
[P0269] The buildings of an age reveal the character of the generation and of its people. When we behold the mighty walls of Jerusalem, we realize that people thought and lived largely in terms of fear and preparedness for war. The ruins of Roman palaces, public baths, and arenas reveal a people of luxury, self-indulgence and paganism. On the contrary, when we visit the great cathedrals to which the world still makes pilgrimages, we behold the living manifestations of an age of faith and devotion to God. Moreover, every building should express the purpose for which it is to be used, whether library, school or factory. Hitherto church architecture in America has had no definite type, indicating that it was exclusively designed for religious purposes. When our Pilgrim forefathers called the church the "Meeting House" they were correct, for it was the only place for every public assembly, whether civic or religious. But today the average community has many meeting houses:-the civic auditorium, the public library, and many social and fraternal clubs. This leaves to the church the single field of religion, and in the specialized life of today we should accentuate in every possible way the fact that the church is primarily a temple of religion. If architecture can manifest a specialized purpose, it follows that the church should be built not merely to be a House of Worship in name, but should express in form and design the divine purpose for which it was erected. In other words, the structure itself should be recognizably distinct from every other kind of building. It should stand in the community as something apart from ordinary things. "It is like a flag: it speaks without words." It is a sermon in stone.
[P0270] The appropriateness of the general Gothic type for church building-its capacity to illustrate truth-consists in the fact that it represents better than any other form, the nature and spirit of man reaching upward. The dominant characteristic of the Gothic is the pointed arch. The round, or Roman arch, is finished-it speaks of the finite. The Gothic arch points upward to the Infinite. It speaks of the universal spiritual aspirations of humanity. The spire probably originated as a landmark to guide distant caravans to their destination. But as time and experience have touched many a physical thing with spiritual beauty, so the church spire has become a symbol of aspiration. So we see in our Church that the lines of window and arch culminate in the slender spirituelle fleche, which reaches like a hand toward God, and by its skyward direction seems to liberate the soul from the earthconfining atmosphere of material things.
[P0271] Our age is intoxicated with the discovery of its power to control the material-the power of mind over matter. But more marvelous still is the power of material things, brick, stone, or wood-when moulded by art, to speak of great truths. How clearly can they be made to body forth that superiority which the human soul can exercise over nature. The church is a self evident symbol that the life of man consists of something more than material things. It declares that there is more in life than food and drink, more than that which we can touch, taste and see. So every upreaching arch and window and spire reminds us that the great facts of life are mysteries bordering on the infinite —that the great forces are not steam and electricity and radio activity, we can live without these-but that the real indispensables are vision, faith, and love. Our age greatly needs this message. The material world presses in upon us from every side, and the peril of the present is that the soul will surrender to its domination. Therefore the Church Visible is a message of God calling this materialistic civilization to see that only the spiritual will ultimately endure, and that the soul which rises up to God, is greater than the greatest skyscraper or the greatest railroads, greater than the greatest air-fleets or the greatest world dreams or anything else material. Standing in the very heart of the city, where the race of man goes by, it is a symbol and reminder of the fact that "man cannot live by bread alone".
[P0272] The exterior also speaks of that simplicity in which our forbears gloried. Its brick, in modest alternating colors, reflects the common dust; its parapets set forth the familiar emblems of the Cross, the Bible and the Chevron. The doors express the welcome which Christ and the Church extend to all, high or low, rich or poor, truly a House of Prayer for all people. These ideas of the primacy of the spiritual, of simplicity, of aspiration and unity do full honor to the spirit of our Pilgrim antecedents, and to the growing ideas that are dominating the mind of today. When we enter the doors, whose flutings are engraved with the rambling vine and grape, we behold in them the beautiful symbol by which Christ, in the communion service, taught that the Way to goodness, power and greatness lies through self-effacing service. As we walk beneath this arbor of vine, it seems to say, "let all who enter here be ready to minister, not to himself, but to minister, and use his life for the betterment of humanity.
[P0273] The Interior
[P0274] Legend says that the early Christian of northern Europe met in the forest aisles for a religious service. Whether true or not "the long drawn aisles and fretted vaults," the groined ceiling spanned with fan-like arches suggest branches of trees meeting overhead. The vestibules, as we pass through them, accentuate the soaring height of the interior that stretches away before the eye and gives us, as we pause, the sense of large spaces and of great distances. When we stand and behold the glittering arches, curving upward, and the broad diaphanous band of color, extending along the length of the ceiling, and when we behold the dominant color of gold, the color of glory, we feel the spell with which God, through the beautiful, works upon the hearts of men.
[P0275] We feel the atmosphere of awe and of reverence which induces the religious frame of mind. Though there may be no message from the pulpit or peal from the organ, voices speak in many a heart that which it cannot speak for itself. For when we have striven to the utmost to express the mystery of the universe and the depth and the greatness of our human lives by spoken word and hymn, then art, music and atmosphere speak unconsciously to the heart, expressing the inexpressible for which there is no language. Who can measure the influence of the interior which impresses us through the subconscious mind, particularly through the silent moments preluding the formal service?
[P0276] The various features of the interior convey separate messages. The baptismal font which, according to history, should properly stand at the door of the church, ushers the child into the fellowship of Christ and of His disciples. It proclaims the truth that the child should grow up within, and not outside of the church. It is the first of those many steps which should bind the growing child to the Christian cause with ties that cannot be severed. The windows through which the light of day ever shines express the principle of the open mind that we preach,-open as the day to all the facts of God in nature and revelation, and welcoming every fresh gleam of truth. It was not intended that we should grope in darkness. God is Light, and pours it upon every dark problem of earth. There is light enough for us to discover the Father's will and to walk in His shining way. The general brightness and richness of color which lights up everything represents the note of joy and gladness which the gospel-the good news-was first to sound. In certain scenes in the windows there is the suggestion of a gaiety and mirthfulness that reminds us of the play of humor which in parable and sermon flashes forth from the radiant personality of Jesus.
[P0277] The humble pew which has entirely escaped attention has an ancient and honorable history. It harks back to the age when the family and not the individual was the unit of religion and of civilization. The pew was built to be large enough for the average family, and every family had its allotted place. When we look back over the centuries, we behold father, mother, sons and daughters, sitting together in that unity which binds the family together with everlasting bonds, and establishes it as the basis of organized society.
[P0278] Historically the chancel itself represents the truth that Jesus Christ is the Head of the Church, and the nave the body of believers. Architecturally everything centers upon it as the focal point, the Communion table is the symbol of His Presence, and the central panel of the Memorial Window portrays Him as standing among the great figures of the Bible, receiving the natural homage which His Person evokes. This is an impressive representation of Christ. There is a majesty of character and a kingliness of spirit that helps us to understand the spell which He cast over His disciples and which still rules the ages.
[P0279] There are many features of the chancel which speak a varied language. The position of the pulpit, set to one side, expresses the idea that preaching is not the only function of a religious service. The Church is more than a hall with a platform for a speaker. Today, books, magazines and the radio impart knowledge. "We are long on information, but short on meditation."
[P0280] We have a little time for that wise passiveness in which the soul can commune with itself. The modern world wants more than much speaking; it craves a sanctuary of silence where, in the healing presence of the Eternal, it may sense the things that make for its peace. The pulpit will always be an evangelical necessity and a spiritual power, but those other elements,—art, music, symbol,—help to create the spirit of worship, the religious frame of mind. In silence and speech, in instruction and meditation, in worship and education, we shall find the fullness that was in Jesus Christ.
[P0281] The special reading desk for the Bible, proclaims its exclusive position, as the source of Truth. Whereas the Bible contains the universal Gospel, the pulpit gives the interpretation of the Minister of the day. The position of the Minister, and the choir is also instructive; their seats do not face the congregation, but are placed at the sides, indicating that they themselves are part of the congregation. This position expresses our belief that all-the Minister, the singers, the congregation-stand upon a common footing in the presence of the Invisible, and worship in a common spirit and for a common end.
[P0282] The cross, carved in the rood beam and displayed in other places, is the historic emblem of faith. The cross was an ancient instrument of execution. To the cultured Greek and Roman it was an offensive thing as St. Paul says. But it was not the manner of Jesus' death on the cross that is significant. The great fact is the divine spirit of His whole life that ended in an heroic death. Because of this, the cross is the symbol of faith, love and sacrifice, those sublime virtues of the soul, in whose practice lies our salvation and which can never be outgrown even on the highest plane of Being.
[P0283] The Communion Table, under the Memorial Window, is a symbol of that unity of spirit which love creates between Christ and His disciples. The Beatitudes upon either side of the window declare the positive note of our faith. We believe that Christianity is essentially a way of life, and that those who follow that Way will find life to be a blessed beatitude.
[P0284] But after we have sounded these varied notes, we have not yet beaten all our music out. As life is full of mystic lights and shadows which our human reason cannot explain, so in the Church there broods the intimation of mystery. Especially in the chancel where the shadows linger, there is suggested to the mind the things that cannot be seen. "There are dim things in life which we can never explain, but God is in them all. It is not necessary that all should be made manifest in God's House. These are very little houses that we build for Him, unless they suggest something that goes beyond what we see.
[P0285] We cherish the hope that every one who looks upon our Church will be able to see that it is a Church built upon the foundation of the Apostles, of the Prophets, and of the Martyrs, Christ Himself being the cornerstone.
[P0286] The Church House
[P0287] The furnishings of the Church House have been made to conform with the architecture as much as possible without following any particular period. The assembly rooms are all curtained with English Casement Cloth in a natural color, and the velour curtains which are used on the street floor to divide the large assembly room into class rooms are of a darker shade but a neutral color. The assembly rooms have all been furnished with a handsome long table in a dark walnut and two highbacked carved armchairs done in a dull bluish-green velvet. The other chairs are all of dark walnut finish, Windsor type.
[P0288] The Kindergarten department has chairs of the same design and finish but in a small size. Low matching tables, seating about twelve children, are to be used. The curtains are of glazed chintz on a natural back-ground showing an amusing pattern taken from an early American map. There are reds and blacks used in the design and the curtains are bound with the natural color corded with red.
[P0289] The Conference room or Chapel and the Minister's study adjoining it, are carpeted alike in plum colored carpet having a small all-over design in a darker shade. The curtains of the same casement cloth are trimmed with a heavy yarn fringe showing blocks of natural color and the green-blue, which is the color of the marble in both fire-places. The conference room furniture consists only of a long walnut carved table, and of the same type, high-back arm-chairs done in the blue-green.
[P0290] The study has a pair of walnut bookcases against one wall. Across the room is a handsome carved walnut chest which conceals a steel filing cabinet. A walnut table desk is placed near the windows at the front, and beside the fire-place is a large armchair done in a blue-green leather. Two small arm-chairs, done in a colorful printed linen of English design, bringing out the greens and plum colors and shading to deep rose, complete the furnishing of this room except for the lamps with their parchment shades and a small low table for books.
[P0291] The two rooms on the second floor, which are reserved for the women's activities, have been carpeted alike in a soft blue green with an all-over pattern in a lighter tone. The curtains in the South Parlors are of printed linen in a Grinling Gibbons design. The back-ground is the color of the carpet, and there are large bunches of fruit and flowers in dark green, purple and crimson, with a little dull gold. This has been used on two armchairs, and the two sofas are done in dark purple velour which makes a good back-ground for the pillows done in plain satins and in the prevailing colors. There is a small English Barrel shaped sofa done in crimson damask placed near a beautiful high carved chest of dark walnut. At one end of the room is a pair of console tables, placed at either side of the door. Upon these are jars of dull blue-green pottery for flowers. At the opposite end of the room is a fire-place with a tapestry hanging above it, and there are two small Jacobean tables holding lamps of English design with mica shades. In front of the fire-place is a bench done in a plain Feize, and at either side a pair of Barrel chairs done in crimson damask. Two Jacobean arm-chairs, done in Bargello patterned wool tapestry and trimmed with a heavy wool fringe, and a very beautiful carved Tudor table, complete these furnishings. The table contains a pair of rough pottery lamps in a deep gold color which have matching parchment shades.
[P0292] The North Parlor has been amply furnished with things of a lighter type which can easily be moved about. The curtains are of English crimson rep, trimmed with a wool fringe combining this color and the blue-green and the natural color of the wall. The furniture, consisting of small sofas and chairs, is enameled a dark dull green and covered in the printed linen like the curtains in the adjoining room. There is a Gate-leg dark oak table of English design which is large enough to use as a tea table if needed.
[P0293] The dining-room has folding tables which can be stored away, and the chairs are the same walnut finish, Windsor type, in use elsewhere in the Church House. The curtains are of linen gauze, the color of the walls, and are striped at intervals in bright jade green. They are simply made, with headings and deep hems of matching plain green glazed chintz.
[P0294] Household duties bore heavily upon the spirit of Martha, and she rebelled. On the other hand the Bible does not tell us that Mary lightly regarded the Art of Kitchen Cuisine, because she had access to any conveniences. It was a difference of temper, but modern science has been able to transform many a Martha into a Mary by providing her with labor saving devices. The modern Church seeking to minister to all the needs of its people provides adequate kitchen and dining room equipment. The large Parish hall beneath the Church will provide a dining room ample for the greatest affairs.
[P0295] In the kitchen the steam table with Monell top, the baker's table equipped with the essentials for baking, the Hobart mixer and the Majestic coffee urn, will greatly simplify the preparation of dinner. Then after the dinner is over, and the program is about to begin, a very audible sigh of gratitude will rise from every woman's heart for the electric dishwasher. The labors of love and sacrifice of woman in the cause of Christ have no parallel, and in memory of this, we most solemnly dedicate these chosen vessels of service unto the Lord.
[P0296] Memorials and Gifts
[P0297] It has been the custom for Christian people to perpetuate the memory of loved ones by means of suitable memorials in the form of carved wood, window or furnishings. These not only add to the beauty and dignity of worship, but they also tend to inculcate deeper feelings of reverence for the House of God and a sense of nearness to those who are justly held in remembrance. We have many memorials, and hope that others may be given, which will help to make our Church a shrine haunted by Unseen Presences.

[P0406] Historical Sketch
[P0407] It has been generally acknowledged that the Congregational and Presbyterian Churches of Kalamazoo had a common origin. At the seventy-fifth anniversary of our Church in 1910, Rev. H. W. Gelston, minister of the First Presbyterian Church, spoke on "Our Common Origin" and Rev. W. B. Dickinson spoke on "Our Common End".
[P0408] The history of our Church is associated with the early beginnings of Kalamazoo. In 1831 Titus Bronson settled here with his family, and the village grew until in 1832 it had one hundred inhabitants. The first religious services, according to a copy of the Kalamazoo Telegraph of May 4, 1852, were held in connection with a union Sunday School in a schoolhouse east of the present site of the Capitol Theater. This school had twenty-five scholars and five teachers and was composed of Methodists, Congregationalists, Presbyterians and Episcopalians. On June 5, 1835, a church was organized as a Presbyterian Church, consisting of some of these same elements, for it states that the Methodist element withdrew early and organized a Methodist Church. The Congregational and Presbyterian members continued together, remaining in connection with the St. Joseph Presbytery until 1849. Mr. Kedzie states that it was purely Presbyterian until January, 1841, when it adopted a Rotary session. In January, 1842, it adopted the Congregational form of church government. In June 1849 it united with other churches. to form the Kalamazoo association of Congregational Churches. The division of these two congregations, which had worshipped together since 1835, came in 1848. In February, according to the "Telegraph" the "subject of erecting a newer and larger church was agitated and events followed which caused the division." At a public meeting called to consider this matter the question was raised by Judge Webster, whether it was to be a Presbyterian or Congregational house of worship, whereupon Judge Hinsdale remarked that if it were to be a Presbyterian building he would not contribute one cent. Acting upon human nature, Mr. McCall said: "If that is the understanding, I will not give one cent to a Congregational building". Then about fifty Presbyterians withdrew and erected a new structure. The Congregationalists kept possession of this old edifice-the Church erected in 1835-until 1850 when they made plans to erect a larger and more imposing building. In September, 1852, the second Church, of more dignified architecture, with stately entrance of Georgian style and large belfry surmounted by a lofty spire was erected. It was built of brick its dimensions being fortyfour by seventy and was located on Church Square. Such, however, was the growth of the congregation that enlarged accommodations were made several times. A chapel was built in 1857, twenty-six by thirty-four, at the rear of the Church for social, library and Sunday School purposes, and in 1868 a gallery was extended partly around the Church. Such was the energy of the Church that in the spring of 1869, sixty-nine members were dismissed to organize the Plymouth Congregational Church on the southwest corner of South Park and West Cedar Streets. In 1884 this Church was disbanded and many members re-united with the first Church.
[P0409] The early history of Kalamazoo and views of early days were also disclosed by periodicals and newspapers found in the corner stone box. There was a Michigan Telegraph of June 23, 1852, a Kalamazoo Gazette of August 2, 1852, and the Nederlander of August 6, 1852, which was Kalamazoo's Holland newspaper. A letter written by Rev. A. S. Kedzie and found in the box reads, "There are in this village today six Protestant churches and a Catholic church. There is a Baptist church with one hundred seventy-six members; a Methodist with eighty members; St. Luke's with fifty members; a Dutch Reformed with sixty members; a Presbyterian with one hundred eighty, and the Congregational with one hundred sixty. There are four common schools, one and sometimes two private schools, a Female Seminary, and a Baptist College and Theological Seminary, each in the early stages of their growth. How changed will be the conditions of this place (now numbering between three and four thousand inhabitants) and of this church when this shall be brought forth for the perusal of some distant generation. May He then look down from the heights of Heaven and see Freedom and Christianity over-spreading the earth."
[P0410] In another note, A. S. Kedzie wrote in 1851, "It may not be amiss to preserve in this box a notice of the Ladies' Library Association which was formed in the winter of 1851. It is composed of ladies of all the different religious societies. Each pays 25c a year for the use of the library. Gentlemen pay $1.00 a year for the same privilege. The ladies and gentlemen of the Association spend one night of each month together for a literary chat."
[P0411] The Concert Casket
[P0412] Among the treasures found in the corner-stone box when opened after the fire of 1926, there was an unique pamphlet with a black cover called the "Concert Casket". This was published by the Sunday School, January 1863. The editor states that this magazine appeared every two months. It gives us an insight into the early life of the Church and contains poems and brief essays by different members of the school. The "Birds Nest" was a notable class for many years, its influence still abiding in the memory of many and demonstrates the influence which the Christian teacher exercises over his or her pupils. A poem by one of the pupils pays this tribute to Mrs. Montague, the teacher.
[P0413] The first and last verses follow:
[P0414] "Merry little birds are we
[P0415] Gathered in one precious nest;
[P0416] Weekly fed with crumbs by thee,
[P0417] Oh how happy, oh how blest!
[P0418] And when from thee we all must part
[P0419] On earth to meet no more,
[P0420] May we each other welcome next
[P0421] Upon the shining shore."
[P0422] There was also an essay about another kind of birds-"jail birds", who were imprisoned for theft, the essay teaching that every child should avoid any form of dishonesty, even to taking a trivial. The writer also says, "I have been told that there are some boys in this village who are guilty of throwing stones at old people."
[P0423] The "Concert Casket" closes with the story of one of the Sunday School young men who was killed in a cavalry charge in Mississippi and was buried underneath a tree upon which a comrade carved his name. So says the editor: "One day all will die; some will die this year. Are your prepared? If not, this day come to Jesus."
[P0424] The Manual of 1885 reveals great religious activity. There was an Industrial School, a Union Aid Society, three Missionary Societies (Home, Foreign, Young Women)—a Willing Worker's Band and a Bible Reader's Union. The "Mosaic" was a semimonthly literary and social society for young people. "The Ten Bands" were the one hundred ten young Christians and converts divided into ten bands for mutual help in the Christian life. A weekly prayer meeting was maintained from the time of the organization of the Church. In April, 1885, the membership, including absentees was four hundred twenty-six.
[P0425] The Church having become large and influential, it was decided at a special meeting April 25, 1887, to erect a new building. The corner stone was laid July 3, 1889, and the spacious and magnificent church which burned in December, 1925, was dedicated June 22, 1900. At these exercises Dr. W. A. Waterman said: "This new structure will be one of the most commodious and elegant edifices in the State, and the citizens today should feel justly proud of their heroic undertaking."
[P0426] An accompanying bit of history put in the strong box states that the exercises were characterized by the simplicity with which our Pilgrim forefathers inaugurated the religious history of our country. It was also stated that in 1889 there were eighteen Churches, one college, a female seminary and a private boarding school-Mrs. H. W. Sheldon's. The population was was about twenty-two thousand and the chief products were carts, carriages. and celery.
[P0427] At this time the Honorable Otto Ihling was mayor of the city; Cyrus Luce, Governor of Michigan; and the Honorable Benjamin Harrison, President of the United States.
[P0428] The dedication booklet of this last Church carried this item: "This Church will be opened Monday evening, June 23, at six o'clock for the selection and rental of pews." Among the stated activities were two Bible study classes, one Tuesday and one Wednesday evening; a vesper service Saturday at 7:30 P. M., in addition to the midweek meeting. The current expenditures, for the year 1892, were over seven thousand dollars, including one thousand dollars for music. The indebtedness, the interest on mortgage and small items, were one thousand forty-two dollars and twenty-nine cents. The plate collections for the year were four hundred forty dollars—a large sum at that time. 
[P0429] The history of the past forty years of this Church with its turrets and majestic tower, its beautiful windows and its vine clad walls is still fresh in the memory of many. It opened its doors to many notable gatherings. It has been the scene of great events and a noble array of men and women have worshipped beneath its lofty vault and served within its beloved walls.
[P0430] How appropriate the words of the poet—
[P0431] "We make her goodly battlements
[P0432] And her foundations strong,
[P0433] We hear within the solemn voice
[P0434] Of her unending song."

[P0445] This third Church was destroyed by fire the noon of December 29, 1925. The flames had gained such headway before the discovery that it was a total loss. The conflagration created a sensation in the community and was a shock to the congregation. The Church, however, rose immediately above the disaster, a building committee was appointed and authorized to select an architect and to secure preliminary plans without delay. During the interim the Church accepted the offer of the People's Church for its temporary services. In these homeless years our congregation, undismayed by its handicaps and its heavy burdens, continued to progress, proving that you can "burn a Church but you cannot kill its spirit." And so upon the ashes of the third Church, magnificent in its proportions and with scarcely an equal in the State, has been built this new structure which will be a "thing of beauty and a joy forever". The corner stone was laid April 24, 1927.

[P0458] The new Church is thoroughly modern in every respect, with facilities for every need. It has a chapel near the Park Street entrance for weddings, conferences and other spiritual offices. On the other hand, it is equipped with every modern social convenience, and enjoys the scientific heating system of a Silent Automatic.
[P0459] Its unique character, with lofty nave and chancel, and long ambulatories, affords infinite possibilities for a morning service of deep spiritual impressiveness, and for an evening service of dramatic power. The Church now stands at the high water mark of the years, one of the two or three strongest churches of the city, with a constantly growing membership. It now has nearly one thousand members, including the non-resident. Its Church School is organized into departments, following the graded system with total enrollment of five hundred, including the Cradle Roll. It has also formed the habit of ending the year with a balance in the Treasury. And our crowning triumph, the Church is dedicated free of debt. And now as we confront the future, we know we have a plant that can grapple with the complex problems of the future, and we shall be able to render a service beyond the dreams of our fathers.
[P0460] As a fitting climax to these noteworthy events, a story is adapted to the occasion.
[P0461] "In the early years of the twentieth century a new Church was built in Kalamazoo, Michigan, by the people who called themselves Congregational, which has been justly famed since as one of the most beautiful churches in the Country. Many reasons have been given for its wonderful beauty.”
[P0462] “It is herein stated that this Church was not built to order by a few men as so many ready made churches of the time, but that every man, woman and child in the whole parish helped to build it, there not being a single one who leaned back and said, 'Let others do it.'”
[P0463] "Every one, rich and poor, young and old alike brought of their very best. The calls for money were festivals of generosity when even the most liberal surprised themselves and the more prudent ones blossomed out into glorious prodigality of cheerful giving.”
[P0464] "Out of the generous love with which these people loved their new Church before it was built, there came to life this worldfamed sanctuary built not so much out of clay, wood and glass as out of the love and self-sacrifice of a whole generation.”
[P0465] "That is why in our prayers we thank God for the Twentieth Century. That is why many journey even from afar to say their prayers within it, and after many years amid the multitudinous ruins of other churches, it will remain a glorious inheritance."

[P0520] Social and Service Activities
[P0521] The spirit of friendliness has always animated our Church, and the warm tints that suffuse its walls are designed to express our cordiality of feeling. The many organizations that work in our midst seek to maintain old friendships, and to introduce newcomers into our Church family. The Women's Union which has had a long and glorious history of service, is now at its height. The Mayflower Guild was organized for a similar purpose among the young married women, and is an effective working force for the Church. The Chancel Committee was recently organized to meet the needs of the Chancel and the choir of the new Church.
[P0522] There are other organizations which have, probably, no known parallel. The Pilgrim Club for both husbands and wives was the pioneer in this field and has a beautiful record of loyalty, fellowship and service. The Plymouth Club for young married people of the Parish enjoys semi-monthly metings for mutual acquaintance and personal culture. The Bachelor Club provides opportunities, semi-monthly, for wholesome entertainment for single young men and women. The Men's Club seeks to inspire the spirit of brotherhood among our men, and to become the dynamic for every forward movement in our congregation.
[P0523] The Fidelis Club is a group of young business women who hold monthly meetings for service and fellowship. The Young Married Couples' Class is building up a growing group for the Church School, drawn from that great group of young married folk in our Parish. The student work has become a permanent part of our Church, partly directed by the State Convention. It is becoming a stable institution to maintain the faith and loyalty of the Congregational students of our local Colleges. The Scrooby Club is a new name for the former Christian Endeavor and has become a large and effective society. The Intermediate Society, recently organized, is supplementing the morning sessions of the Church School for that age. It is our aim to co-ordinate into one unified School of Religion all of the Sunday morning and Sunday evening hours for the Christian education of our youth. For the recreational life of our Church School, and for our young people, we offer the Parish Hall, and the several rooms on the first floor of the Church House.
[P0524] The Church also believes in maintaining the Thursday evening tradition with changes adapted to modern life. On the first Thursday of the month there is a conference for the Church School faculty and the Board of Religious Education, while on the second Thursday young people gather. Upon the third Thursday a church dinner will be given to which every family is invited-it is Fellowship night. On the fourth Thursday is a monthly conference of the Deacons, the Deaconesses and the Church Committee. Every fifth Thursday is reserved for a Missionary supper, followed by an illustrated program, showing the progress of the Gospel in the non-Christian world. This is directed by the Missionary Society, which strives with tireless efforts, to nourish the passion of Jesus for the redemption of the whole world.

[P0596] Our Appreciations
[P0597] Mr. Aymar Embury II, our architect, ranks among the noted architects of America. His qualifications for the task to which he has devoted himself are not only artistic, but also literary. Among the books he has written is "The Early American Churches", which is a standard work upon that subject. Among his many achievements are the First Congregational Church of Springfield, New Hampshire, six of the college buildings of Princeton University, and the new buildings of Wesleyan College Middleton, Connecticut. In all he has completed some six hundred specific buildings, many of which are noted. His relation to our building enterprise has been not merely professional but intimately personal. Our church is the child of his heart as well as of his brain. He has given his time and talents to the building in an abundant and overflowing measure.
[P0598] We are also indebted to Mr. O. F. Miller, our contractor, whose interest in the Church has been a labor of patience and sacrifice, and to whose conscientious devotion, unsparing pains and willing co-operation our Church has been built upon material foundations which cannot be shaken.
[P0599] The Church House has been furnished in the spirit of the whole Church, in order to have the exterior and interior one harmonious unity. The chairs and the tables have a Gothic note and the carpets, tapestry, and curtains give a colorful character to the whole. Miss Eleanor Austin has achieved this artistic task.
[P0600] The designs and color scheme for the Sunken Garden were supplied by the landscape artist, Mr. William Pitkin, of Cleveland, Ohio, with suggestions from our architect to conform to his general plan.

[P0616] What Is It All For?
[P0617] A church is something more than brick and stone, buttressed walls and a soaring spire, something more than a colorful interior, carved walnut and stained glass. The Church is more than a beautiful architectural design.
[P0618] The building is only the body, the soul of which is the spirit of the people. The congregation is the real Church, and only as there flowers in our lives the spiritual beauty, of which the visible Church is but a material symbol, will it fulfill its purpose, and justify the labor and sacrifice of its builders. Let us pray that we may carry out the designs of the great Architect of the universe, whose purposes are revealed to us in nature and in Scripture. Let us cultivate the symmetry and grace of character, the earnestness of spirit and the zeal for the Kingdom of Christ which shall make of us living stones in the Temple of God.
[P0619] To Him we dedicate this earthly structure for His Worship and Service through the coming generations.
