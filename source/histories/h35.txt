[P0021] For a period of one hundred years, the First Congregational Church has had a definite part in the development of Kalamazoo. That its history has been one of honor and progress brings a sense of satisfaction and joy to its present members, who are now engaged in celebrating in a fitting manner a century of good works.
[P0022] Historical facts of Kalamazoo's beginnings are interestingly and vividly told in the History of Kalamazoo County, published in 1880. In the early 1800's, Michigan west of Detroit was considered nothing but a swamp, this information gaining headway through a statement of Judge Holmes, a government surveyor, who wrote a friend, "It is nothing but a swamp, I know, for I could not survey it except in winter on the ice."
[P0023] Who the first white man was who saw the fertile and beautiful valley of Kalamazoo, history does not record, but he probably was some fur trader, hunter or courageous missionary, trekking his slow way through the woods and up the streams. The great Northwest Territory, as far as Michigan was concerned, was virgin territory and still the home of Indian tribes.
[P0024] During the war with Great Britain from 1812 to1815, a blacksmith establishment was kept in the vicinity for the benefit of the Indians and it is said to have been established and supported by British military authorities. By 1823, we find a fur trading post in the valley and the white man had made the entering wedge.
[P0025] In 1829, Titus Bronson, coming with his family from Ann Arbor, founded the village of Bronson on the Kalamazoo river. The earliest settlement in the county was at Prairie Ronde, established in November, 1828, by Bazel Harrison, but Bronson is undisputed as the first settler of the village of Bronson later to be called Kalamazoo.
[P0026] Bronson came into Michigan from Summit County, Ohio, a few miles east of Akron. Whether or not he was originally a "Connecticut Yankee" as some supposed, the records do not tell. Moving from Ohio, he settled first in the western part of Wayne County. He did not remain there long, however, moving in 1824 to Washtenaw County near Ann Arbor where apparently he thought the soil was better adapted to the crop he raised extensively, potatoes.
[P0027] In 1826, he went for a time to Ohio, returning in 1827 again to Ann Arbor and visiting the Kalamazoo Valley the same year. After one more trip to Ohio, we find him in Michigan again, making his way with wife and one child to Kalamazoo. In June, 1829, he established the village which he called Bronson, building his first cabin on a space near the corner of Church and Water streets.
[P0028] The next year he built a house on a site near what is now Bronson Park, having set aside that land for village purposes.
[P0029] The Formative Period 1835-1849
[P0030] Five years after the founding of the village of Bronson, a village then of over a hundred people, we find members of four religious denominations meeting in a union Sunday School. This Sunday School was organized on May 4, 1834, and its members were Presbyterians, Congregationalists, Methodists and Episcopalians. They met in a schoolhouse on East South street near Henrietta, the present site of the Capitol theater. The School had twenty-five pupils and five teachers. The Sunday School organized in 1834 formed the nucleus for the organization of the church on June 6, 1835, formed from Congregationalists, Presbyterians and Methodists.
[P0031] The church was organized with 14 members: James and Mary Porter, Cornelius and Mary Northrup, Abraham and Charlotte Edwards, Martin and Hulda Huydenburk, John and Deana Everard, Mrs. Ruth Edwards, Miss Ann Edwards and Titus and Sally Bronson. The Methodists soon withdrew to join the Methodist church already organized in 1833. The trustees of the new church were Martin Huydenburk, Luther Trask, Alexander McCall and John Everard.
[P0032] Mr. Henry Montague and Mrs. Abigail Montague, who gave many years of splendid service to the church and Sunday School, where Mrs. Montague conducted the famous "Bird's Nest," joined the church in 1837, the second year after it was organized.
[P0033] In March, 1836, the name of Bronson village had been changed by an act of the legislature, to Kalamazoo. Titus Bronson, hurt and disappointed, soon left with his family and never returned.
[P0034] Church meetings were held for some time in the schoolhouse where the Sunday School had been organized, but by the summer of 1836 a small church was built on the south side of South strect, between Rose and Burdick, just east of the present Library building.
[P0035] "It was a small frame building 24x40 feet, with a square belfry. There was an outside entrance into a narrow vestibule, the width of the building, one end of which was finished for storing wood for the large oblong box stove, situated in the center aisle. Two doors entered into the auditorium. There was a platform at the north with steps at either end, and between the two doors was the pulpit on which was a bright red velvet cushion and a Bible of the same shade." There was no musical instrument. The song leader used a tuning fork and "lined" the hymns.
[P0036] The new church prospered and grew in numbers, making a fifteen foot addition necessary in 1841. The church was organized under the Presbytery of St. Joseph, but each member was allowed great liberty of belief. In 1841 a Rotary session was adopted. In January, 1842, it adopted the Congregational form of government but retained its connect on with the Presbytery until 1849.
[P0037] The church, organized by Revs. Humphrey, White and Jones. had for its first pastor Rev. Silas Woodbury whose pastorate continued until August, 1839, when he resigned because of ill health. He was succeeded in 1840 by Rev. O. P. Hoyt.
[P0038] It was in the early forties that dissension in the union church appeared, adoption of the Congregational form of government being perhaps the first sign that difference of opinion had arisen. It was not until 1848, however, that things came to an open break.
[P0039] February of that year a meeting had been held to discuss the building of a new church, the old church having become inadequate to the needs of the congregation. At this meeting, discussion turned to heated argument and the break occurred.
[P0040] We quote from an old report of the meeting: "The question was raised by Judge Webster whether it was to be a Presbyterian or a Congregational house of worship. Whereupon Judge Hinsdale remarked that if it was to be a Presbyterian building he would not contribute one cent. Acting on human nature Mr. McCall said, ‘If that is the understanding, I will not give one cent to a Congregational building'!" The following year, 1849, the Rev. O. P. Hoyt who had been pastor since 1840, withdrew with 45 members and formed the First Presbyterian Church, the Congregationalists retaining possession of the old church building.
[P0041] Church Reorganization and the Building of the Second Church 1849-1890
[P0042] A decision was soon made by the Congregationalists to build. a new and larger church, since the congregation grew in numbers in the two years following the division. The site of the present church, the north-east corner of Academy and Park streets, was chosen as a site and the new church soon started. The old church was sold for $1,000 to the Dutch Reformed Congregation, of which Rev. Wynandt Gardiner was pastor. The building was later moved to Rose street just north of Main where it was used as a blacksmith shop as late as 1883. While the new church was being erected, the congregation worshiped in the Court House.
[P0043] The new church, of simple colonial style, was built of red brick, rectangular in shape with long narrow windows on the sides. As is characteristic of most Congregational churches of New England, it had a square belfry tower surmounted by a steeple. The door was of Georgian architecture, dignified and stately.
[P0044] The corner stone of the new church was laid September, 1852, and the church was dedicated on February 24, 1853. The building committee was composed of N. A. Balch, Martin Wilson and Silas Trowbridge. The building firm was Dennison and Krause.
[P0045] Rev. A. S. Kedzie was pastor, having been installed as pastor in May, 1850. At the time of the dedication the church had 161 members. Mrs. Kedzie, wife of the pastor, was the first president of the Ladies Library Association.
[P0046] An interesting glimpse back to the Kalamazoo of that day is given in an editorial of the Kalamazoo Gazette, dated August 6, 1852. We quote the editorial comment: “The amount of building in our village this summer exceeds that of any previous season. Something over twenty-five dwellings have been erected, or are in the process of erection. Besides these, McNair's new brick block, the Fireman's Hall and the Congregational Church, will be three of the finest buildings in the interior of the state. The Fireman's Hall in size and finish will be fully equal to its prototype in the city of Detroit. If there is anything to prevent Kalamazoo from growing rapidly into the consequence and dignity of a city, we fail to discover it. Five years we predict will find us numbering 8,000 inhabitants." These were not quite prophetic words, although Kalamazoo in 1857 was a good-sized town. The population in 1860 numbered 6,070.
[P0047] A letter written by Rev. A. S. Kedzie and placed in the cornerstone of the church being built in 1852 and opened when the church was torn down in 1888, gives some interesting facts about Kalamazoo. His letter states: "There are in this village today six Protestant churches and a Catholic church. There is a Baptist church with one hundred and seventy-six members; a Methodist with eighty members; St. Lukes with fifty members; a Dutch Reformed with sixty members; a Presbyterian with one hundred-eighty and the Congregational with one hundred sixty. There are four common schools, one and sometimes two private schools, a Female Seminary, a Baptist College and Theological Seminary, each in early stages of their growth. How changed will be the conditions of this place (now numbering between three and four thousand inhabitants) and of this church when this shall be brought forth for the perusal of some distant generation! May He then look down from the heights of Heaven and see Freedom and Christianity overspreading the earth."
[P0048] In another note he writes: "It may not be amiss to preserve in this box a notice of the Ladies Library Association which was formed in the winter of 1851. It is composed of ladies of all the different religious societies. Each pays twenty-five cents a year for the use of the library. Gentlemen pay one dollar a year for the same privilege. The ladies and gentlemen of the Association spend one night of each month together for a literary chat."
[P0049] We see from Mr. Kedzie's letter that Kalamazoo in the space of twenty-three years had grown from a village of some one hundred people to a town of almost four thousand. The land boom of 18341836 had given the village a boost in population which had kept up a steady growth from that time.
[P0050] 
[P0051] Mr. Kedzie remained the pastor of the church until May 1853, when his relation as pastor was dissolved by advice of council.” Rev. Granville Wardwell was called to the church to succeed Mr. Kedzie and was elected pastor in February, 1854. He remained only one year, leaving in February, 1855.
[P0052] Rev. Edward Taylor was the next pastor, being installed in February, 1856. An interesting event of his pastorate was the organization in 1856 of the first Kalamazoo County Sunday School Association. The organization meeting was held in the Congregational Church.
[P0053] M. B. Miller, clerk of the church from 1867-1896, tells of events which followed the dedication of the church in 1853. He writes:
[P0054] "... such was the increase of the congregation that enlarged accommodations became necessary in 1857, the house being lengthened by an addition of twenty feet. At the same time a chapel, 36x24 ft., was built."
[P0055] Not many records remain of early members of the church yet a few were outstanding in personality and devotion.
[P0056] Mr. and Mrs. Henry Montague, joining in 1837, and Mr. and Mrs. A. T. Prouty, who joined in 1842, were among the early members.
[P0057] Mrs. Mary Brownell, who joined in 1856, went to the south as a missionary of the American Missionary Association at the close of the Civil War. Mrs. Harriet Seeley also joined in 1856, Mrs. Susan Cobb in 1857, Mr. and Mrs. Hayden and Mrs. Mary Beckwith in 1859. Mrs. Beckwith was a tireless worker, calling on every new family when they moved to Kalamazoo. If they were not affiliated with any church, she invited them to attend the Congregational Church.
[P0058] Mrs. Mary Dean also joined in 1859. Mrs. Amanda Mills joined in 1860. Miles B. Miller, who joined in 1860, should be mentioned with special interest, having served the church as clerk for twenty-nine years, from 1867-1896.
[P0059] In the cornerstone of the church which burned in 1925 an interesting pamphlet with a black cover was found called the "Concert Casket." This was published by the Sunday School in January, 1863, and appeared every two months. It contains poems and stories written by members of the Sunday School.
[P0060] Rev. Edward Taylor remained pastor until October, 1863, and was followed by Rev. Henry Mills who was installed as pastor in April, 1864.
[P0061] In the years following the building of the church, the country was aflame with talk and argument about the slave question. Very definite sides were taken by the churches, Congregational churches uniformly being bitterly opposed to slavery and doing everything possible to bring about freedom for the slaves. In the "Christian Press" of Friday, July 23, 1852, we find an interesting article valiantly upholding the Congregationalists of the West for their position in opposing slavery and a scathing criticism of the editors of the "Evangelist" for accusing the church of the "heresy of not communing with slave holders," calling this non-fellowship with slaveholders "an element of unsoundness" and stigmatizing it as "comeouterism."
[P0062] The Congregationalists were berated also for upholding the New School of Theology as advanced by Mr. Finney of Oberlin College. Just how radical these teachings were we have not been able to find out, yet knowing of the courage of some of the teachers of that time, we know that if they felt it wrong to hold human beings in bondage they would have had no hesitation in saying so.
[P0063] That a real spirit of courage and a fine sense of justice permeated most Congregational churches of the time, we do not doubt and we find evidence that members of our own church were outspoken in opposing the evils of slavery.
[P0064] Mrs. Montague, one of the early members of the church and a loyal and devoted member always, was zealous in helping the escaping slaves to freedom. Several members of the church at the present time remember Mrs. Montague and her black friend, a former slave, Sojourner Truth, who sometimes came to church with her and told of her slave days.
[P0065] Sojourner grew up a slave in New York state. Her real name was Isabella Van Wagoner, the last name being that of her master. At the age of forty she became free under the act of the New York legislature in 1817. Possessed of great powers of speech, she spent the remaining years of her life traveling over the East and Middle West, telling the pathetic story of her girlhood and young womanhood and preaching the evils and cruelties of slavery. The name, Sojourner Truth, she took when she began her journeys. She died in Battle Creek, Michigan, in 1883, mourned by black and white alike. Mrs. Montague was always her friend and helped her in many ways.
[P0066] On June 30, 1861, Kalamazoo County sent out its first regiment to the Civil War. The women of the church helped during the war years as women all over the country helped.
[P0067] About this time, in 1863 or 1864, an infant class in the Sunday School was organized by Mrs. Montague, mentioned before, a woman of fine and noble character, being especially interested in the religious and moral education of children.
[P0068] There were one hundred children in the class. The collection was taken up in two baskets which contained real bird's nests, which gave the name "Bird's Nest" to the class. One of the baskets was used for collecting money for the school, the other for the needy.
[P0069] The children repeated together these lines:
[P0070] "We bring the bright pennies,
[P0071] They are little we know,
[P0072] But love going with them.
[P0073] To dollars they grow.
[P0074] And much as this surely,
[P0075] We children can see,
[P0076] That were there no pennies
[P0077] No dollars there would be!"
[P0078] If a child brought only one penny, it invariably went into the basket for the needy. In November of each year these pennies, often amounting to many dollars, were used to purchase material which was made into garments for the poor. On the first Sunday of each year, these garments were exhibited in the chapel of the church that the children might see what they had accomplished. Mrs. Montague often took some of the children with her, when she distributed these garments to the needy people.
[P0079] The establishing of the Bird's Nest bank is an interesting story. One Sunday in February, 1864, a soldier visited the "Bird's Nest" class. When the plate was passed, he handed out a penny, saying, "There is a penny I found in the bottom of my pocket, and it won't grow there. Now I want to deposit it with the Bird's Nest and see if it will not grow there." Shares in the bank were sold for ten cents. Soon, people in every state in the Union and in several countries bought shares. The soldier's penny grew to a fund of $1,000 which Mrs. Montague gave to the Freedmen Cause. One of the certificates representing a share in the bank and issued to Ezra Nichols, is now in possession of the church.
[P0080] The "Bird's Nest" was a vital force in the Sunday School for thirty-four years, during which time Mrs. Montague was the teacher. At a church service in 1892, after she had had to give up the class, every one in the congregation was asked to rise who had been a member of the "Bird's Nest." "It looked to one newcomer," commented Mrs. James Hatfield in relating the events of that day, "that nearly the whole audience arose. Near the end of her life, although she had been an invalid for several years, she was brought to church in a wheel chair, and she once more pronounced a benediction upon the little children."
[P0081] Of Deacon Montague, many fine tributes have been given. Mr. J. L. Stratton, writing of him, says: "He was a noble man, an able man of outstanding gifts. He could have been governor of the state if he had asked for it."
[P0082] Rev. Mr. Mills remained pastor of the church until October, 1865, being followed by Rev. F. F. Ford. He became pastor in November, 1865, and left in January, 1867.
[P0083] Rev. O. S. Dean was the next pastor, assuming the pastorate in July, 1867. He was a man of great brilliance and exceptional ability and was greatly beloved. For many years after he left, people still spoke of him and of his influence and power over them. He was the grandfather of the late Col. Dean Halford, whose mother, Marie Dean, spent her girlhood in Kalamazoo
[P0084] Mr. Dean remained pastor until November, 1873. It was during his pastorate in the winter of 1869, that fire greatly damaged the church. The church was repaired and redecorated during the year and a gallery was built, partly around each side. An organ was placed in the gallery.
[P0085] An important event took place in the spring of 1869, when thirty-nine members were dismissed to organize the new Plymouth Congregational Church on the south west corner of Park and Cedar streets.
[P0086] Plymouth Church had it's greatest period of growth during the pastorate of Dr. Burton, a man of great strength of character and a fine pastor. He was greatly beloved by his entire congregation. The church, in its fifteen years of existence, grew in membership, having 250 members at the time the church burned in 1884. Rev. Mr. Sanderson was pastor at this time.
[P0087] The church building was of simple lines, though not of strictly colonial architecture. It was surmounted by It was surmounted by a steeple, and had a small lecture room or chapel at the back. This part which was saved at the time the church burned was later used by the school board for the pupils of the ninth grades at the time the old Union school was torn down to make room for a new building. One pupil in that school who attained some renown was Henry Brown, who later became chaplain of the Rough Riders.
[P0088] In the late sixties and early seventies, we find many familiar names on the church roll. Leroy Cahill, Mr. and Mrs. Julius C. Burrows, later Senator from Michigan, Mr. and Mrs. Henry Weimer, Mr. and Mrs. O. M. Allen, Henry Hobbs, Anna Cobb, Mr. and Mrs. L. M. Gates, Mrs. Josephine Shafer, Allen D. Chappell, Mr. and Mrs. Barendreight, Mr. and Mrs. Ashby, Mrs. Mary Latta and Mrs. J. R. Jones. These are names closely associated with the early life of the church, names of members who always gave generously of their services and whose lives, because of this devotion have blessed ours. Mr. W. O. Jones, whose mother was one of these early members has attended the church since 1872-joining in 1892. Mrs. Harvey Cavanaugh, who joined in 1873 and Mrs. Helen Gilles, who joined in 1875, hold the longest record of membership in the church today.
[P0089] In 1874 extensive improvements were again made to the chapel, it being lengthened. A recess was made in the rear of the pulpit for the organ and the platform extended, giving space for a choir. In 1880 the main part of the church "was redecorated and reseated and in the fall of 1883 the chapel and library underwent extensive repairs and refurnishing."
[P0090] Rev. Frank Russell followed Rev. O. S. Dean as pastor being installed in May, 1875 and resigning in April, 1878. He was followed by Rev. W. H. Thomas, who became pastor in September, 1878 and continued as pastor until December, 1880.
[P0091] Little historical data have been found concerning the life of the church and its members in relation to the community during the late 60's and 70's, but from accounts of extensive improvements and additions to the church in 1857, and again in 1874, and redecorating at different times we are led to believe that it was materially prosperous. That the church was also vitally interested in its spiritual progress is attested by the letter of M. B. Miller who tells us of a number of revival services, mentioning specifically those in the winters of 1856, 1858, 1860, 1868, 1870 and again in 1876, when many new people came into the church.
[P0092] Dr. C. O. Brown, a teacher at Olivet, came to the pastorate of the church in February, 1881, and was installed the following April. He has been described as an eloquent speaker and during his pastorate in the winter of 1884-85, a revival was held which was a "deep and pervading work."
[P0093] During his pastorate many large contributions were given to the church and to missions. Notable givers at the time were Mr. and Mrs. W. A. House, in whose memory the beautiful stained glass window was placed in the church which burned in 1925.
[P0094] Mrs. House, always a generous giver to missions, left a bequest of $7,000 including her home, 445 West Michigan Ave. to the Woman's Home Missionary Union of Michigan as residuary legatee. The home was purchased by the Y.W.C.A. and used until the new establishment was built on Rose Street.
[P0095] Mr. Leroy Cahill was a generous giver to missions also, as was Mr. Timothy Hudson, who supported two missonaries with the American Sunday School Union, organizing Sunday Schools in destitute places of the West.
[P0096] Another member of the church, helpful and influential in its affairs during the 80's, was Mr. S. A. Gibson, who started the first paper mill in Kalamazoo. He was a man of noble character, one of the rare spirits who combined good business sense with an innate sense of justice and a fine sincere friendliness which helped smooth over many difficult events in the church. It was he, who always "poured oil on the troubled waters," an admirer said while relating some of the affairs of the church during this period.
[P0097] It was during Mr. Brown's pastorate in the spring of 1884 that Plymouth church burned. For various reasons it was decided not to rebuild it. Plymouth church was disbanded and most of the members went back into the First church. That same year several of the public buildings burned, giving rise to the feeling that the buildings had been set afire.
[P0098] During Mr. Brown's pastorate also the church celebrated it's fiftieth anniversary in 1885. An interesting item concerning that event is supplied by Mr. John L. Stratton of Ottowa, Kansas, a faithful and enthusiastic helper in the church and an efficient superintendent of the Sunday School for seven years. He writes us: "At the Fiftieth Anniversary Celebration a Dr. Taylor, one who was an early pastor of the church, was the oldest one present. (Dr. Taylor was pastor from 1855 to 1863). He was asked to administer the communion. Before he dispensed the bread, this was his prayer.
[P0099] It is easy to remember.
[P0100] 'Oh Lord, the soil has been torn and turned by the plow,
[P0101] The seed has been buried out of sight,
[P0102] It has sprung up to newness of life,
[P0103] It has been separated from the chaff in a machine,
[P0104] It has been ground to powder in a mill,
[P0105] It has been thrust into a heated oven
[P0106] To come forth the symbol of Thy bruised and broken body.
[P0107] Take it and bless it to our use'!"
[P0108] The fiftieth Anniversary was celebrated in a fitting way by two days of meetings held in the church. The church was fortunate to have three former pastors present, all of whom took part in the services. They were Rev. Frank Russell, pastor from 1874 to 1878, Rev. Edward Taylor, 1855 to 1863 and Rev. A. S. Kedzie, pastor from 1850 to 1853. The membership at this time was 426. In 1885 the church, under the direction of Ernest Bourner Allen, Arie Binkhorst and Albert Hitchcock, organized the East Side Sunday School. Ernest Bourner Allen, later to become one of the great Congregational ministers of the country, was its first superintendent. Rev. Arie Binkhorst, now of Olivet College, was its first secretary and treasurer. The Sunday School first met in the old East Side schoolhouse. Later the church bought a lot on the corner of Gilbert and Charlotte avenues and built a small chapel.
[P0109] Mr. Schrier donated his labor for the building of the chapel. A group of young people from the church went over every Sunday afternoon to teach classes. Mrs. Chilson became superintendent of the school and was succeeded in 1891 by Mr. James Hatfield, who remained superintendent until the school closed in 1894 or 1895.
[P0110] When the school was organized a number of young people from the Methodist church attended and helped, but later when the East Side Methodist church was built they withdrew.
[P0111] There was not room at that time for two schools. Sentiment in the Congregational church was very strong in opposing the continuance of the school in view of the fact that the church was heavily in debt, so the chapel was closed in 1895 and sold in February 1897, for $109.80, the building being turned into a dwelling. Many people in the church felt however, that the closing of the chapel was a lost opportunity for Congregationalism in Kalamazoo. The money received from selling the property was given to the Michigan Home Missionary Society.
[P0112] The manual of the church dated 1885 tells of great activity in the church and Sunday School. Of the women's organizations, the Union Aid Society, The Women's Foreign Missionary Society, organized about 1875 or 1876, a Home Missionary Society organized in 1884 and a Young Women's Missionary Society were the chief organizations and were busy working for the good of the church.
[P0113] The Willing Workers Band and a Bible Reader's Union and the "Mosaic," a literary and social society for young people were other organizations of influence at this time. Dr. Brown's pastorate ended on the last Sunday of December in 1885.
[P0114] On May 18, 1886, a unanimous call was extended to Rev. William A. Waterman, who became the pastor in June of that year. In March, 1887, articles of incorporation of the church were read and adopted at a meeting of the membership in order to "elect trustees and hold property without the intervention of a separate society."
[P0115] Plans for a new church were talked over soon after Rev. Mr. Waterman became pastor, but it was a year before any definite action was taken.
[P0116] Of the needs for a new church we refer again to Mr. Miller's reports. "The large accessions to the membership in the spring of 1885, (after Plymouth church burned), proved to be a blessing there was literally not room to receive. The seating capacity of the building became entirely inadequate to the wants of the church and congregation . . . There seemed to be no thought that the present building could be made to answer the need. A new edifice seemed the only alternative . What disposition should be made of the present building became a serious question. It's material precluded its being removed bodily. It could not be sold to advantage.
[P0117] It seemed, . . . like sacrilege to demolish it, but it had served its day and though it had done it well, it had now become entirely inadequate, and with gratitude for its past usefulness and regret at parting with it, the decision was arrved at, that it must give place to more spacious quarters."
[P0118] In April, 1887, a meeting of the church was held to consider the building of a new church. After full discussion a finance committee was made up of Dr. Hitchcock, John Gilmore, O. M. Allen, S. A. Browne and W. N. McCourtie. Of the forty-five members on the committee for plans, many were influential people in the church and city at that time. Among them were Mr. and Mrs. Leroy Cahill, Mr. and Mrs. Latham Hull, Mr. and Mrs. J. W. Osborne, L. A. Pratt, J. L. Stratton, Mr. and Mrs. O. M. Allen, G. Vandekreeke, D. B. Merrill, Mrs. Henry Beckwith, Senator Julius C. Burrows, Mrs. Mary Weimer, Mrs. Haydin, Mrs. Ranney and Mrs. House. These committees continued to work on plans for the new church for a year.
[P0119] Early in 1887, the church sponsored the opening of a mission Sunday School in the southwest part of the city. It was located in the octagonal house at the head of Minor avenue on Westnedge avenue then called West street.
[P0120] Prof. Richards, principal of the High School, and Arie Binkhorst started the school. It was not thought of as a future church proposition but simply as a neighborhood service. Mr. VandeKreeke became superintendent. Miss Ida Hobbs and Miss Emma Low were active in the school.
[P0121] Many of the young people in the neighborhood received their start in Sunday School and church in this school. Although the membership was not large (about 100), the work was successful and much regret was felt when it was given up in April, 1890.
[P0122] In April, 1888, another meeting of the church was held to further discuss plans for building. At this time Dr. H. O. Hitchcock, chairman of the finance committee, reported $26,000 pledged toward a new building, conditioned on the sum of $30,000 being raised before the beginning of the building. A motion to proceed with the raising of that amount was made and adopted. "The required amount was speedily pledged." At a subsequent meeting of the church the building committee was instructed to proceed with demolishing the old church and building the new, not to exceed in cost "$40,000 exclusive of organ and carpets." S. J. Osgood of Grand Rapids was the architect. Mr. Leroy Cahill, who became chairman of the building committee after the death of Dr. Hitchcock in December, 1888, was assisted by William McCourtie, John Gilmore, L. C. Pratt, David Turnbull, H. M. Beckwith, S. A. Browne, William H. Johnson, O. M. Allen, L. M. Gates, and Peyton Ranney as the building committee. D. B. Merrill, a member of the Board of Trustees, was influential in working out the plans for the new church.
[P0123] The farewell service in the old church was held on Sunday, April 14, 1889. An account of the service is given in the Kalamazoo Daily Telegraph of April 15, 1889. "It was a glorious Sabbath day for the farewell services. When the last organ voluntary struck up, the old instrument sent its swelling notes resounding from wali to wall against which they will resound no more." The service was impressive, the congregation saddened by the thought of giving up the church where so many events of historical significance and spiritual experience had taken place, yet uplifted by the thought of the newer, greater church that was to come. The pulpit furniture and Sunday School Library, gifts of Mr. O. M. Allen, were taken from the second church to be used in the new church.
[P0124] During the interval of building, services were held in the old Episcopal church at the corner of Main and Park streets, where the Y.M.C.A. now stands.
[P0125] On July 3, 1889, the cornerstone of the new church was laid. The service, conducted by Rev. W. A. Waterman, pastor of the church, was simple. Rev. A. S. Kedzie, minister of the church dedicated in 1853, was present and took part in the service.
[P0126] In the cornerstone were placed copies of various papers published at that time: The Congregationalist, The Advance, The Beacon, My Reminder, a copy of the Kalamazoo Telegraph and Gazette, a copy of the last Manual, a copy of each Missionary magazine, one copy of State Minutes of 1888, the annual report of the Women's Home Missionary Union, a photograph of the second church, a photograph of Rev. Mr. Waterman, some coins and a list of subcribers to the new church. Mr. Waterman's address records for us some interesting historical data as that of Mr. Kedzie had in 1852. He writes: "The city has today (1889) eighteen churches, one college, one chartered female seminary, one private young ladies boarding school (Mrs. H. W. Sheldon's), and about 22,000 inhabitants (the population in 1852 was between three and four thousand). The chief products are carts, carriages and celery. It publishes two papers, the Telegraph (Republican) and the Gazette (Democratic).
[P0127] The First Congregational Church publishes the paper My Reminder. Hon. Otto Ihling is Mayor of the city. Hon. Cyrus Luce is the Governor of Michigan. Hon. Benjamin Harrison is President of the United States."
[P0128] The church membership at this time was 455, the Young Peoples Society had 85 members and two successful Mission schools were being conducted by the church. The membership in the Sunday School and Mission schools at this time totalled 643.
[P0129] Third Church is Dedicated 1890-1928
[P0130] The church was dedicated June 22, 1890, with impressive services and entered with high hopes into a new period of progress and usefulness. It was a spacious church, suggesting with its tall square tower and corner turrets, the period of Byzantine architecture. The interior contained a large auditorium with a gallery around two sides making a seating capacity of one thousand; a chapel and double parlors opening off the auditorium and separated by sliding doors increased the seating capacity at the time of special services.
[P0131] Mr. Waterman remained as pastor of the church until June, 1891. He is described by Mr. Frank Fuller in his historcial sketch of the church from 1890 to 1927, as a man of "force and ability, possessed of character and initiative and his work here as pastor was felt for many years."
[P0132] The building of the church became a real burden to the congregation. Renting of the pews took care of the pastor's salary, but nothing more. It was a larger church than was needed, yet it was felt that the growth of the congregation would prove the wisdom of building the larger church.
[P0133] In October, 1891, the constitution of the church was rewritten, a new form for admission of members and a new Covenant of Faith were adopted.
[P0134] Mr. Waterman was followed by Rev. Caspar Wister Hiatt, who became pastor in April, 1892. He was a young man of interesting personality, of brilliant mind, was an exceptionally able speaker and was greatly beloved and admired. He drew large audiences to the church and made a serious attempt to lower the church debt. Five members of the board of trustees pledged $1,000 each, on condition that the rest of the amount be raised. This effort failed, unfortunately. Mr. James Hatfield became chairman of the Board of Trustees in this year, holding that office for sixteen years and fulfilling his duties with a devotion and loyalty which was an inspiration to all.
[P0135] It is easy to realize how friction in the church developed at this time when we read the report of the Treasurer for the year 1892 and that of Mr. L. M. Gates, chairman of the committee on repairs. The church was heavily in debt and added to the burden was a leaky roof! In view of the fact that the church was only two years old, one cannot blame the members for being discouraged. The poor construction of the roof and the constant repairs needed was a scource of friction for several years.
[P0136] In spite of these heavy burdens the church progressed in its various activities. The Women's Foreign Missionary Society was a strong force in the church and the Home Missionary Society, organized in 1892, was successfully carrying duties assigned to its members. The Sunday School had a period of great activity during these years, one of its interesting features being an orchestra, members of which were from the Forbes family. The young people's Christian Endeavor had a membership of 100, and the Junior Endeavor, organized in 1892 or '93, soon grew in membership to 50. Ida Van Zant was the leader of this group.
[P0137] A large class of young men, high school, business and college students, clerks and others, had been formed by Miss Richards, later a missionary to Africa. Mrs. James Hatfield became the teacher in 1892, and she relates interesting events of the class.
[P0138] "Late in 1892, one Sunday, a group of young women announced they had come to join the class. A controversy arose but was soon smoothed over. The superintendent, Mr. Henry Oggel, suggested that it be called the Young People's Bible class and that we move to the gallery in the rear of the church where there would be more room and a chance for freer discussion. The attendance in the class was at times between 40 and 50. As the 'Bird's Nest' was in the front seats of the church, some of the clever minds dubbed ours the 'Crow's Nest.' Out of that 'Crow's Nest' emerged faithful homekeepers, a young woman who is at the head of an important department in one of our largest stores, a nurse who is giving her life for the amelioration of suffering, school teachers, two college professors, a Detroit journalist, the surgeon general of the Chicago and Northwestern R.R., a circuit judge who has held office in Kalamazoo eighteen years, and many successful citizens scattered through many states."
[P0139] In the clerk's book under date of December 8, 1892, we find an interesting testimonial to the loyalty and faithfulness of Miles B. Miller, clerk of the church. The testimonial, in the hand writing of Mr. Hiatt and signed by him, reads: "At the close of the midweek services on recommendation of the Standing Committee, a complimentary vote was tendered Mr. Miles B. Miller, in view of his faithful and valuable services as clerk of the church for the past twenty-five years; in which vote it was also ordered, that the sum of twenty-five dollars, be handed Mr. Miller as a slight token of the general esteem in which he is held. The vote was by rising, and was heartily unanimous."
[P0140] Mr. Hiatt left in September of 1893. He had a successful and notable career as a minister in Peoria, Illinois, the American church in Paris, France, in Cleveland, and again in Peoria. He was succeeded by Rev. Thomas E. Barr, a former pastor of the Presbyterian church of Kalamazoo, in November, 1893. Mr. Barr was installed as pastor in November, 1894. He was a man of fine intellectual attainments and a good speaker. He drew good audiences to the church but was not able to help in diminishing the church indebtedness to any extent. He remained pastor for three years until October, 1896, when he left Kalamazoo to take the pastorate of a church in Milwaukee. The women's societies were reorganized in 1893 as the Women's Union.
[P0141] Rev. R. W. McLaughlin became the next pastor in May, 1897, being installed December 20, 1898. He, too, was possessed of intellectual attainments, was a fine speaker and a good organizer. The church was still in debt, and the roof still leaked. Mr. Fuller writes, "He found the membership somewhat disunited and affairs disorganized. However, he took hold earnestly and soon the debt was pledged, the membership reunited and the course of events running along smoothly."
[P0142] Dr. McLaughlin was a bachelor when he came to the Kalamazoo church but was ably assisted by his mother, a woman of charm and ability. The church prospered under his leadership. He married a Kalamazoo girl, Miss Anna Oggel, daughter of Mr. and Mrs. M. Oggel. Dr. McLaughlin was pastor of the church five years, until October, 1902, when he was called to Park church in Grand Rapids, later going to a Brooklyn church where he remained twelve years. He returned to Kalamazoo in June, 1910, to give an address, "The Church and Democracy—a Survey," at the time of the seventy-fifth anniversary of the church.
[P0143] It was during Dr. McLaughlin's pastorate that Mrs. Bowman joined the church. She had had a wonderful experience, when as a young woman in 1858 she had made a trip to Hawaii in a sailing vessal to marry Mr. Lorrin Andrews of Maori, Hawaii, who established the first boarding school for boys in the Hawaiian Islands. After her husband's death she returned to the states and later married Mr. Bowman.
[P0144] It was told of her that she always had two pockets in her dresses, one of which she claimed was the Lord's, and into which she put one-tenth of all her money so that she might be ready at any time for a missionary or church appeal. During the pastorate of Dr. McLaughlin, the church purchased a lot on the southeast side of the city with the idea of starting`a mission church. Strong opposition to the project arose, however, and the lot was sold before a church was built.
[P0145] During Dr. McLaughlin's pastorate, also, the Young Women's Auxiliary was organized. Under the leadership of Nellie Sawyer Clarke, the society became a strong group in the church.
[P0146] Rev. Howard Murray Jones followed Dr. McLaughlin, becoming pastor of the church in September, 1903. He came from Berea College, Kentucky, where he had been a teacher. He was a man of kindly disposition, of fine ability, a great worker and a good speaker. During his time as pastor, the church made great progress, spiritually and materially. Reports of the annual meeting held in October, 1905, tell us of further repairs on the roof and outside of the church totalling $1,676.00 and of redecorating the inside.
[P0147] A fine new organ was purchased for the church during this year also, through the earnest efforts of Mr. and Mrs. Jones, costing $6,250.00. On October 22, 1905, the organ was dedicated at an impressive service. Clarence Eddy gave an opening recital on Friday, October 13, and $250.00 was raised to help on the organ debt. Mr. Eric Delamarter, assistant director of the Chicago Symphony Orchestra at the present time, played at the dedicatory service. Other changes in the church were made at this time, the dining room and kitchen in the basement abandoned, and a better equipped kitchen installed upstairs, the chapel being used as a dining room when needed.
[P0148] Mrs. Jones, a woman of fine organizing ability and a distinctive and delightful personality, was a great help in the conduct of church affairs. One of her dear friends, on being asked to speak of Mrs. Jones' character and personality said, "She was wonderful, and everything that a woman ought to be."
[P0149] To Mrs. Jones must go much of the credit for securing the fine new organ for the church and for planting the ivy vines which in a few years time covered most of the church walls, softening and beautifying the contour of the building with their lovely green foliage.
[P0150] During the pastorate of Rev. Howard Murray Jones, the “Marthas" organized. It was an organization of women of the church who raised money in various ways in order to help the unfortunate and destitute. The Plymouth club, a club of men of the church and later identified with the Sunday School and the Pilgrim club, a social club to promote friendly relations in the congregation, were also organized.
[P0151] After a pastorate of six years, Mr. and Mrs. Jones left in April, 1909. The church was in fine condition, the membership being a little over 600.
[P0152] Rev. Walter B. Dickinson, a young man of scholarly attainments and of charming personality, was installed as pastor in June, 1909. He was an excellent preacher, a good administrator and church affairs progressed under his pastorate. Mrs. Dickinson endeared herself to members of the church with her quiet manner and friendly spirit.
[P0153] During these years the church had a steady growth. All of the organizations in the church were active, the women's organizations assuming some of the church obligations. In the first year of Mr. Dickinson's pastorate, a revision of the membership roll brought the number down to 500. This figure remained about the same for several years, but did not indicate any lack of perseverence on the part of the organizations within the church.
[P0154] The Sunday School enrollment averaged over 300 and collections in the school for 1913 amounted to $392.82; missionary contributions in 1913 were $1,281.91.
[P0155] The Plymouth club and the Pilgrim club were both active. One of the interesting events of Mr. Dickinson's pastorate was the celebration of the 75th anniversary in June, 1910. Among the speakers on the program of that occasion we read familiar names: Mayor Charles Farrell, Senator W. R. Taylor, Chase Osborn and Dr. McLaughlin, pastor from 1897 to 1902. The services extended through five days with special music featuring each service.
[P0156] Mr. Dickinson left Kalamazoo in February, 1913. He has since made a notable contribution to the activities of the National Recreation Association of America.
[P0157] Rev. William Campbell succeeded Mr. Dickinson. He was a young man, a sincere Christian gentleman, was a keen thinker and a good preacher. He came as pastor in October, 1913, remaining until November, 1915. The church membership increased under his leadership to 536.
[P0158] Rev. Augustine Jones came as pastor in December, 1915. He remained only about one and a half years, being forced to resign in May, 1917, because of ill health. He was a good speaker also, and possessed a pleasing personality. He was ably assisted by Mrs. Jones in the work of the church. In 1916 the church was redecorated, and a new cork floor was laid in the auditorium.
[P0159] In 1917 the United States entered the World War. Until the conflict ended the women of the church joined hands with other women of the country in working for the soldiers.
[P0160] The Women's Union organized the first hospital unit for the war at their own expense, before the Red Cross took up the work. Mrs. S. O. Bennett was the efficient chairman. They continued to work as a unit of the Red Cross, meeting every Wednesday for the entire day at the church.
[P0161] From the Congregational Church, it is said 36 young men entered the service. The tablet commemorating the "Gold Star" boys burned with the church in 1925.
[P0162] It is interesting to note that the record of Kalamazoo County citizens who took part in the world conflict was written by a member of the Congregational church, Mrs. O. H. Clarke, ably assisted by Kenneth Payne, also a member of our church.
[P0163] Rev. J. Twyson Jones followed Rev. Augustine Jones, beginning his pastorate in September, 1917. Dr. Jones, a man of fine dignity and kindly disposition, remained as pastor six years until April, 1923. He was a scholarly preacher and successful in keeping the many activities of the church progressing. Mrs. Jones was of great help during these years in the women's organizations and young people's societies. Possessed of a friendly nature, her quick sympathy and power of understanding people, endeared her to all.
[P0164] During Dr. Jones' pastorate, another important step was taken in church affairs, when on February 17, 1918, a business meeting of the church was held following the church service for the purpose of re-incorporating the church under the provisions of Act 53 of the Public Acts of 1901, entitled "An Act for the Organization of Corporate Congregational Churches." More than thirty years had elapsed since the church had received its charter in March, 1887, and it was thought desirable to be re-incorporated.
[P0165] The Sunday School and other organizations of the church under the stimulating influence of Dr. Jones were active in assisting in war work during these years as well as charitable work at home, sending gifts to soldiers, "adopting" French orphans in a few cases, helping the Red Cross in its work and after the war was over contributing at one time $1,100 to the Near East Relief.
[P0166] In 1921, the fine custom of giving each child who graduated from the Primary department a Bible was revived in the Sunday School. This custom was carried on for several years by Mrs. W. O. Jones, whose daughter, Patricia, is now distributing these gifts in memory of her mother.
[P0167] The Plymouth Club-reorganized about 1920 from the Plymouth class in the Sunday School-became an active social club of the young married people under the leadership of Dr. Jones. Dr. Jones left Kalamazoo in April, 1923, to assume the pastorate of a church in Owosso, Michigan. He remanied there several years, and after retiring from active ministry returned to Kalamazoo where as Pastor Emeritus, he and Mrs. Jones are now contributing to the life of the church.
[P0168] Rev. Torrance Phelps became pastor in December, 1923. The church had been without a pastor for eight months, but during that time Rev. John Perrin had faithfully worked to hold the members together.
[P0169] Dr. Phelps brought energy and enthusiasm. "He realized that he had a difficult task," writes Mr. Fuller, "yet he had vision to see the possibilities and set to work to make a canvass of the field. As a result of his labors, the church has grown in numbers and influence." He was a scholarly preacher and a tireless worker. Dr. Phelps was ably assisted in his work by his wife, a charming woman and a fine musician. She possessed a clear, sweet soprano voice, and helped with her talent at many functions of the church.
[P0170] Under Dr. Phelps' leadership the organizations of the church worked actively. The Sunday School was under the efficient leadership of D. J. Heathcote who will soon complete fifteen years of service as superintendent. The women's organizations and the social clubs grew in numbers. Several new organizations came into being. The Mayflower Guild drew together a band of younger women in the church, as did a little later the Priscilla Guild and the Katherine Carver Guild. Two other social clubs, the John Alden and the Bachelor's club were organized. The Boy Scouts had an active group also.
[P0171] On June 6, 1925, the church celebrated its 90th anniversary. At this service this hymn to the church, wrtten by Ernest Bourner Allen, was sung:

Sutherland, State Superintendent of Congregational Churches offered prayer and Rev. Ozora Davis, President of Chicago Theological Seminary, preached the anniversary sermon.

John Dean, Rev. Herbert House, Rev. Charles Meeker, Rev. John Ten Dyke, RevAlfred Walton, Rev. William Woodward, Rev. Wilto Yonker, Mrs. Bell Richards Bunker, Mr. J. H. [P0199] On December 29, 1925, members of the church were shocked by the news which flashed through the city that their beloved church was on fire. The fire, starting, it was later determined, in the organ control room in the basement, had made such headway by the time it was discovered that there was no chance to save it. The church was destroyed except for the walls, which remained standing. One of the beautiful stained glass windows was left unharmed.
[P0200] While the flames were consuming the building, an invitation was received from members of the People's Church, offering the use of their building for our church services. It was an act of Christian fellowship which many members of our church will treasure as long as they live.
[P0201] Greatly saddened by the loss of the church, within whose ivied walls many of the members had worshipped since its building thirtyfive years before, the members turned to the task of planning a new church. Memories of abiding friendships, of deep spiritual experiences, of earnest Christian service given, lingered in their minds as they thought of the old church and set to work to plan the new.
[P0202] Dr. Phelps, possessed of fine organizing ability and with his usual energy, soon had a building committee appointed. Mr. Robert Staebler, whose experience with school building while a member of the Board of Education, made him a logical choice on the committee, was chosen chairman. Other members of the committee were G. B. Rogers. G. P. Wilson, W. O. Jones, L. W. Sutherland, D. S. Gilmore. Jessie Walton, Dr. W. E. Upjohn, F. D. Fuller, A. B. McDonald, Dr. B. A. Shepard, Mrs. J. W. Osborn, M. H. De Loof, Mrs. E. H. Distin, L. C. Fuller, Harold Upjohn and H. W. Anderson. Mr. Aymar Embury, II, of New York City, was chosen as the architect and several months later had plans for the new edifice ready. In the early spring of 1926, the work of tearing down what remained of the old church began.
[P0203] Construction work under the direction of O. F. Miller, began in the fall of 1926. Building operations were halted during the winter, but resumed in March, 1927. On April 24, 1927, the cornerstone of the new church was laid, members walking over after morning services in the People's Church.

[P0215] The Fourth Church 1928-1935
[P0216] The beautiful church, built of brick and stone, an adaptation of Gothic, ecclesiastical design, reminiscent of both English and French early Gothic, was dedicated on October 28, 1928. Its completion gave not only a church home to the Congregationalists, but gave to the city a gem of architecture "replete with significant historical and churchly features." The copper fleche above the "Glory Arch" reaching its spire to the sky, the chancel, the significant events of Christian history depicted in the windows, the beautiful murals telling the stories of noble Christians of an earlier day, and the artistry of the two memorial windows are a few of the features of the new church which make it a real contribution to the beauty of the city.
[P0217] The two memorial windows should have special mention. The north window, with its lovely iridescence of color, shows in the five large panels the figures of Christ, of Mary his mother, John the Baptist, St. Paul the missionary, St. Peter, and below the adoring angels. The lower panels depict five scenes in the life of Christ; His birth, His baptism, His crucifixion, His rising from the tomb and His ascension. The window was given in loving memory of Mrs. Rachel Babcock Upjohn, by her children, Mr. W. H. Upjohn, Mrs. R. S. Light, Mrs. D. S. Gilmore and Mrs. Dorothy Upjohn Delano. Mrs. Upjohn was a member of the church for many years, an efficient President of the Women's Union and always a devoted worker.
[P0218] The memorial window at the south was given by Dr. W. E. Upjohn and his family in memory of W. Harold Upjohn, who spent many hours with Mr. Embury carefully going over details of construction while the church was being built. This window depicts in its panels of bright blue, the figures of those who through the ages, represent the influence of Christianity upon the world. The figure of Christ in the center is surrounded by other figures whose names have gone down in history. They are St. Paul, John Wycliffe, Henry Ward Beecher, Lyman Abbot, Michael Angelo, Erasmus, Shakespeare, Lord Shaftesbury, Louis Pasteur, Joan of Arc, and Abraham Lincoln. The fine organ placed in the new church was the gift of Dr. and Mrs. Upjohn whose long years of devoted service and generosity to the church and to the city have given them a special place in the hearts of all.
[P0219] Many other fine gifts were given to the church. A record kept by the church gives the name of the donor and the memorial. One of the most interesting of the gifts was the beautiful filet cloth for the Communion Table, woven by monks in the sixteenth century, given in memory of Mrs. Hayden, by her children. Mrs. Hayden gave many years of devoted service to the church, joining in 1859.
[P0220] The beautiful communion service was given by the Walton family in honor of Mrs. Margaret Walton, their mother, a beloved member in the church.
[P0221] The auditorium of the church was filled to capacity on the morning of dedication. "The Ceremony of the Keys", an ancient custom of the church was an interesting feature of the service, the contractor, architect, chairman of the building committee and the president of the Board of Trustees taking part with the pastor, in the service. Special music was arranged for the service by the choir. The dedication prayer was given by Rev. Mr. Sutherland, State Superintendent of Congregational churches, and the dedication sermon was given by Dr. Emerson of the North Woodward Avenue Congregational Church of Detroit.
[P0222] During Dr. Phelps pastorate the church had several assistants who helped with student work, Miss Mary Jeffrey, Miss Florence Cook, Royal Gleason, Harry Endsley, Dorman Post, and Patrick Webster. Miss Gladys Kline is at present the efficient church secretary and Mrs. Marhoff the church hostess.
[P0223] Mention should be made here of the efficient and faithful services of W. J. Bristol who was treasurer of the church twenty-seven years, from 1903 until his death in 1930. He and Mrs. Bristol were devoted members and greatly beloved by members of the church.
[P0224] Dr. Phelps remained pastor of the church until December, 1933, receiving a call at that time from a large church in Pasadena, Cal.
[P0225] He had completed ten years of service as pastor, the longest pastorate in the history of the church, and had almost doubled the membership.
[P0226] In February, 1934, Rev. Robert J. Locke of Evanston, Illinois became pastor of the church and was installed April 19, 1934. He is an able speeaker and has, in this short time, with a fine spirit of friendliness, made a real place for himself in the church. He is capably assisted in the work of the church by Mrs. Locke, a woman of culture and refinement.
[P0227] To live one hundred years is an achievement either for a person or an institution. For one hundred years the Congregational church has lived in Kalamazoo. Its members have helped in the development of the city which has grown from a village of 100 to 60,000. It has grown in membership from 14 to 970. The church has known days of trouble and discouragement, of joy and progress. It has been a power for good and its members are proud of its record of service.
[P0228] May the church live to celebrate many more anniversaries and grow in the spirit and devotion to the Christian ideal of service which motivated those earnest, sincere, Christians in 1835!
